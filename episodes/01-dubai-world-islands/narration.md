## Hook

Four businessmen from Ireland bought Ireland. Not the country. A pile of sand shaped like it, four kilometers off the coast of Dubai, for one hundred and forty-two million dirhams. A year later, a British developer bought Great Britain, and a few months after that he was sitting in a Dubai jail. And in a courtroom in two thousand and eleven, a lawyer told three judges that the whole world was falling back into the sea. Here's the craziest part. More than twenty years after the first island rose out of the water, there's still no power cable running out to them.

## Lock-in

This is the story of how Dubai sold the world before anybody lived on it. You see, the strange part isn't that the plan failed. Lots of plans fail. The strange part is that the islands are still out there, every one of them, and almost nobody has moved in. So how do you build three hundred islands, sell them to some of the richest people on the planet, and end up with a map that's mostly empty?

## Point one · Building a planet out of sand

In May two thousand and three, Dubai's state developer, Nakheel, announced its next idea. It already had a palm tree made of sand sticking out into the Gulf. Now it wanted the entire world. About three hundred islands, arranged so that from a plane you'd see the continents. Europe here, Africa there, a little Antarctica at the bottom.

To make it, dredgers sucked sand off the seabed and sprayed it into place. That's where the first number comes from. Three hundred and twenty-one million cubic meters of sand. That's about one hundred and twenty-eight thousand Olympic swimming pools. Around it all, they piled three hundred and eighty-six million tonnes of rock into a wall, to keep the waves from eating the map.

An island made like that has a catch. From above, it looks finished. Golden beach, blue water, a perfect shape. But nothing is on it. No pipes, no power, no road, no bridge. The only way to get there is a boat. So every island was sold as a blank plot, and the buyer would build the rest.

And the plots weren't small. The whole map sits inside an area about seven by nine kilometers. A single island was anywhere from two point three to eight point four hectares. That's roughly three to twelve football pitches of brand new beach, each one surrounded by water on every side. If you owned one, you didn't have neighbours. You had a horizon.

[GAG: a red supercar rolls off a ferry onto an empty island; the architect steps out in sunglasses and looks around at nothing but sand]
Right. Supercar, sunglasses, private island. So where's the party? Where are all the beautiful people? It's me and one seagull, and the seagull is leaving.

In January two thousand and eight, the last stone went into the breakwater. Nakheel said the reclamation was done, on schedule. The world was built. Now somebody had to live on it.

## Point two · Selling countries

You see, the islands weren't really sold as land. They were sold as a story. You didn't buy plot number forty-two. You bought a country. And buyers loved it.

Think about the dinner parties. "So, what do you do?" "Oh, I own Ireland." And technically, that wasn't even a lie.

In March two thousand and seven, four Irish businessmen bought the island called Ireland for one hundred and forty-two million dirhams. They called the project Ireland in the Sun. Pubs, hotels, homes, a little piece of home in the Gulf. And like most deals in Dubai back then, they didn't pay it all at once. They paid in instalments, because everyone expected prices to keep going up.

That was the whole engine of the city. You put down a deposit on something that doesn't exist yet. A few months later, it's worth more. You sell it, or you borrow against it, and you buy the next thing. As long as prices go up, everybody wins. Nakheel wins, the buyers win, the banks win.

[GAG: the architect leans on a price chart that only goes up, smug; the chart line bends under his elbow and starts to droop]
Prices only go up. That's not a law of physics, by the way. Ask anyone who was there in two thousand and eight.

So picture the scene. Three hundred islands, each one a plot of sand worth millions, and each one bought with a promise of the next payment. Nobody had built anything yet. But on paper, the world was almost sold out. At the end of December two thousand and eight, Nakheel announced that seventy percent of the islands had been sold.

## Rehook

And then, in two thousand and eight, the money stopped. Not just in Dubai. Everywhere. And a city that ran on the next payment found out what happens when the next payment doesn't come.

## Point three · The day the music stopped

When the global financial crisis hit, the first thing to vanish was the buyer. If you'd paid a deposit on an island, and you suddenly couldn't sell it, and the bank wouldn't lend, you were stuck holding a very expensive pile of sand.

By the end of two thousand and eight, the company of the investor leading the Ireland deal was in liquidation. In two thousand and nine, work on the World was suspended. The dredgers had finished, but the builders never really started.

Then came the twenty-fifth of November, two thousand and nine. The government of Dubai said that Dubai World, the giant state company that owned Nakheel, would ask its lenders to wait. To freeze about twenty-five billion dollars of debt until at least May of the next year. The next day, the main stock markets in Europe fell by more than three percent. Because a few weeks later, Nakheel had a three and a half billion dollar Islamic bond to repay.

Imagine you're one of the people who bought an island. You turn on the news, and the company that sold you the world might not be able to pay its own bills.

It came down to the last day. On the fourteenth of December, Abu Dhabi, Dubai's richer neighbour, handed over ten billion dollars. About four billion of it went straight to that bond. Dubai's stock market jumped about ten percent in a day. The city was saved. The islands were not.

## Point four · The man who bought Great Britain

In two thousand and nine, right in the middle of all this, a British property developer named Safi Qurashi bought the island called Great Britain.

You see, he was one of the people still buying. And to secure the deal, he handed over cheques. According to him, they were meant as security while the money moved, not to be cashed. But they were cashed. Three cheques, worth around one hundred and eighty-nine million dirhams, bounced.

In Dubai, a bounced cheque is a crime. In January two thousand and ten, he was sentenced to seven years in jail.

He always said he'd done nothing wrong. In prison, he went on hunger strike for weeks. He was released in two thousand and twelve, and later, Dubai's highest court quashed the rest of his sentence. But by then, the man who bought Great Britain had spent his time on the map behind bars.

## Rehook

So the buyers were in trouble, the company had needed a rescue, and the builders had gone home. You'd think that was the bottom. But the strangest fight about the world was still to come, and it wasn't about money. It was about whether the islands were even still there.

## Point five · Is the world sinking?

Now remember the boats. Every island needed a way to get people there, so Nakheel gave a company called Penguin Marine the licence to run boat transport across the world. Penguin paid a yearly fee for it, about one point three six million dollars. And by two thousand and eleven, there wasn't much world to ferry anyone to. Picture paying more than a million dollars a year to drive boats to islands where nobody lives. Honestly, as a job description, that sounds amazing. As a business plan, less so.

So Penguin went to the tribunal set up to handle Dubai World's disputes, and tried to get out of the contract. And its lawyer said the sentence that went around the planet. The islands, he said, were gradually falling back into the sea. The project was effectively dead.

Newspapers loved it. The world is sinking. It was the perfect headline for a city that had just been rescued by its neighbour.

But here's the twist. Nakheel denied it straight away. It said the islands weren't sinking or eroding at all. The three judges ruled against Penguin. And in December two thousand and twelve, the tribunal ordered Penguin to pay Nakheel ten million dirhams. An expert told the tribunal there was no erosion.

## Rehook

So the world wasn't sinking. But that was never the real problem. The real problem was that it was empty.

## Point six · A map with nobody on it

A few years later, someone counted. Out of three hundred islands, only two had seen any development at all. The only place you could actually visit was a beach club on the island called Lebanon, which opened in July two thousand and twelve. By two thousand and sixteen, around nine islands had something on them.

[GAG: the architect plugs a hair dryer into a palm tree; nothing happens; he tries a coconut]
First rule of island life here: bring your own electricity.

Think about why. The plan was to bring electricity from Dubai through cables under the sea. As of twenty twenty-five, those cables still hadn't been laid. Anyone building on an island had to make their own power with diesel generators. An island with no bridge means every brick arrives by boat. Every bag of cement, every worker, every fridge, every bottle of water. There's no sewer to plug into and no power line to connect. Before you build a single villa, you have to build a tiny town's worth of pipes and cables, on your own, in the sea.

On the mainland, a developer buys land next to a road that's already there. Out here, the road is the sea, and the sea doesn't care about your schedule. Every delay costs money, every boat trip costs money, and the island makes nothing until it's finished. That's why, for so many owners, the cheapest thing to do with their country was nothing at all.

## Point seven · The comeback that keeps slipping

And still, people try. An Austrian developer, the Kleindienst Group, took the islands around Europe and started building something called the Heart of Europe. Hotels, beaches, floating villas.

It was supposed to be finished by two thousand and twenty-six. Then the target moved to the end of two thousand and twenty-seven, with about half the construction done. In October two thousand and twenty-four, the honest estimate was that the islands won't be complete for another decade at least, because getting anything out there costs so much.

Even the success stories don't last. A luxury resort opened on one of the islands in December two thousand and twenty-one. In April two thousand and twenty-six, it closed.

## Closing

So here's what Dubai actually built. It took about five years to make a planet out of sand, and it worked. The rock wall held. The islands didn't wash away, whatever the lawyer said.

The hard part was never the sand. It was everything you can't see from a plane. The pipes, the power, the boat that has to come every single day, and the buyer who has to keep paying when the money stops.

[GAG: back on the empty island, the supercar's wheels spin in the sand, sinking to the doors]
And the supercar? Turns out supercars don't love sand either.

Twenty years later, you can still fly over it and see every continent. Ireland is still out there, four kilometers off the coast. It's just waiting for somebody to move in.
