## Hook

For more than twenty years, the United States government paid people to spy on the Soviet Union with their minds. No cameras, no satellites, no agents behind enemy lines. Just a person in a quiet room, eyes closed, trying to describe a place thousands of miles away. And in nineteen ninety-five, someone finally checked whether any of it had ever helped.

## Lock-in

This sounds like a movie plot. It wasn't. The documents are real, the government has released them, and you can read them today. The program went through scientists, a famous spoon-bending performer, army officers and more than two hundred missions. So how did the most powerful intelligence system in the world end up paying for psychics, and what happened when the answers were finally graded?

## Point one · Why anyone tried

To understand why a serious government would try this, you have to remember what the Cold War felt like. Both sides were terrified of falling behind. If the other side had a new weapon, a new submarine, a new trick, you had to have it too. And rumours were going around that the Soviets were studying the mind. Telepathy. Seeing at a distance. Whatever it was, nobody wanted to be the side that ignored it and lost.

So, in the early nineteen seventies, the question landed on desks that normally dealt with satellites and spy planes. What if there's something to it? What if a person really can see a place they've never been? If it's even slightly true, it's the cheapest spy system ever invented. No rocket, no plane, no risk to anyone. Just a pencil and a sheet of paper.

That's the trap of a big promise. The cost of trying looks tiny compared to the cost of missing out. And once you start, it's very hard to stop.

Think about it from the point of view of the official who has to say yes or no. If you say no, and it turns out the Soviets cracked it, your name is on the decision that let them win. If you say yes, and it turns out to be nothing, you've only lost a small budget. On paper, yes always looks safer.

## Point two · The lab in California

[GAG: a viewer sits with eyes closed, pencil hovering over paper]
"I see something big. Grey. Possibly near water." "That's the Pacific Ocean." "Nailed it."

In nineteen seventy-two, two physicists, Harold Puthoff and Russell Targ, started this kind of research at the Stanford Research Institute in California. Despite the name, it was an independent research institute, and it did serious work for the government. And the CIA's technical office became one of the backers of their psychic experiments.

The idea they tested got a scientific-sounding name: remote viewing. A viewer sits in a room. Somewhere else, a target is chosen. A building, a hillside, a drawing in a sealed envelope. The viewer tries to describe or sketch it. Then someone compares the sketch with the real target and asks: is this a hit?

Picture one of those sessions. A quiet room, a table, a pad of paper. The viewer closes their eyes and starts talking. Something tall. Something round. Water nearby, maybe. A feeling of cold. Then they draw a shape. And the researcher writes everything down, word for word, because every word might be a match.

You can already see the problem. If someone says "something tall, maybe near water," how many places on Earth does that describe?

## Point three · The spoon bender

Then, in nineteen seventy-three, the lab brought in its most famous guest. Uri Geller, an Israeli performer famous for apparently bending spoons and keys with his mind.

For a week in August of that year, Geller was tested at the institute. In a report the CIA later held, he reproduced drawings while sitting in an electrically shielded room. That's the kind of result that makes people sit up. But the same researchers also wrote that their work with him did not confirm his claimed ability to bend metal.

And here's the detail that tells you a lot about the whole story. Months earlier, the researchers had shown a film of their Geller experiments to CIA people. In one CIA memo, two observers who had watched Geller at the institute had serious doubts. They thought what he did might not go beyond what a skilled magician can do. And they worried that the researchers wanted the results so badly that their controls might not hold.

In other words, the very first people from the intelligence world who looked closely at the star experiment were not convinced. They didn't say it was fake. They said it hadn't been ruled out that it was a trick, and that the people running the tests might be seeing what they hoped to see.

That worry would follow the program for the next twenty years.

## Rehook

Because the experiments didn't stay in the lab. They moved into the military. And psychic spying became a job with a desk, a budget and real targets.

## Point four · Psychics on the payroll

[GAG: a job advert on a noticeboard: WANTED, SPY. MUST SEE THROUGH WALLS. LITERALLY.]
Of all the strange jobs the government ever paid for, this one might be the strangest.

Over the next two decades, the program changed names again and again. Different code names, different bosses, different budgets. Military intelligence took it over. In nineteen ninety-one, the pieces were brought together under one name: Star Gate.

Now imagine you're one of the viewers. You show up to work like anyone else in uniform. You get a coffee. Then you sit down in a quiet room, and someone hands you a target number. You don't know what it is. That's the point. If you knew, you could guess. It might be a building in another country. It might be a missing aircraft. It might be a person. Your job is to close your eyes and describe it.

That's not a metaphor. By the program's own count, the unit handled more than two hundred tasks between nineteen eighty-six and the beginning of nineteen ninety-five. Two hundred times, somebody in the intelligence world asked a psychic for help.

And every one of those tasks raised the same question. When the viewer says "I see something round and grey," what exactly is an intelligence officer supposed to do with that?

## Point five · The problem with vague answers

[GAG: the architect closes his eyes and describes something tall with water nearby; the camera pulls back to a lamp and his own glass of water]
See? Anyone can do it.

Here's how a vague answer can feel like a hit. Say the target is a factory by a river. The viewer says, "Something big. Metal. Water nearby. A feeling of noise." The person grading it sees the factory and thinks, that's close. Metal, check. Water, check. Noise, check.

But what if the target had been a ship? Big, metal, water, noise. A bridge? Big, metal, water, noise. A dam? Same again. A description that fits everything fits nothing.

This is the trap every program like this falls into. The people who believe in it remember the hits. The misses fade. And because the descriptions are loose, almost anything can be read as a partial hit, if you want it to be.

In the intelligence world, that's a serious problem. A spy photo of a submarine base tells you how many submarines there are. A vague sketch tells you, maybe, that there's something near water. You can't plan anything around maybe.

And there's another quiet problem. Real intelligence is checked against other intelligence. A photo can be compared with another photo. An intercepted message can be matched with a radar track. A psychic description can only be checked after the fact, once you already know the answer some other way. At that point, you don't need the psychic anymore.

## Rehook

By the mid nineteen nineties, the Cold War was over. The Soviet Union was gone. And a program that had survived for more than twenty years on fear of falling behind suddenly had to answer a much simpler question. Does it work?

## Point six · The grade

[GAG: a report card lands on the desk with one big red letter]
Twenty years of homework, and the final grade fit into one sentence.

In nineteen ninety-five, Congress moved the program from military intelligence to the CIA. The CIA decided to have it checked from the outside. It hired an independent research organization, the American Institutes for Research, to review the whole thing.

In September nineteen ninety-five, the answer came back. And it was brutal. The verdict: no remote viewing report had ever provided information that could be acted on in an intelligence operation. Not once, in all those years.

The reviewers also said that it remained unclear whether remote viewing, as a real phenomenon, had even been shown to exist.

That same year, the program was shut down, and its existence was made public.

Imagine reading that report as one of the people who had spent years in those quiet rooms. Two decades of sessions, sketches and notes, and the final verdict says none of it could ever be used. Not that it was dangerous. Not that it was secret. Just that it didn't help.

## Point seven · Why it lasted so long

So how does a program like that survive for more than twenty years?

Part of the answer is fear. As long as the other side might be doing it, stopping looks risky. Part of it is cost. Compared to a satellite or a spy plane, a few people in a room with pencils are cheap. A small budget can hide in a big one for a very long time.

And part of it is people. The scientists believed in their results. Some of the viewers believed in their gift. And the officers who had championed it had their reputations tied to it. Nobody wants to be the person who admits that twenty years of work produced nothing anyone could use.

That's what makes this story a collapse, not just a strange experiment. The idea itself was tested quickly. The decision to keep paying for it lasted decades.

## Point eight · The files

[GAG: the architect scrolls through endless scanned files on a laptop; his coffee goes cold, then grows ice]
Thousands of pages, official stamps everywhere, and somewhere in the middle, a man describing a hill.

Today, thousands of pages from the program sit in the CIA's public reading room. Memos about Geller. Proposals for new experiments. Budget papers. Session transcripts. Anyone with an internet connection can scroll through them.

Some are typed, some are handwritten, many are half blacked out. There are drawings in the margins and lists of targets identified only by numbers. It's a full paper trail of a government taking an idea seriously for a very long time.

Reading them is a strange experience. Serious government letterheads. Official stamps. Careful bureaucratic language. And in between, transcripts of someone describing shapes and colours from a place they've never seen, while an officer writes it all down.

## Closing

The paperwork was never the weak point. The program was documented in detail. What went wrong was the test. For years, the people inside judged the results themselves, and a loose description can always look like a near miss to someone who wants it to work.

The moment outsiders asked the only question that mattered, could anyone ever act on this, the answer was no. Twenty years of quiet rooms, two hundred missions, and not a single report that an intelligence operation could use.
