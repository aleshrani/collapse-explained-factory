## Hook

Myanmar built a whole new capital city from scratch and moved its government there in two thousand and five, to a place most of the world had never heard of. The new capital covers more than four times the area of London. Some of its main roads are up to twenty lanes wide. And the photos people bring back from those roads often show almost no cars at all.

## Lock-in

The city is called Naypyidaw. It has ministries, hotels, shopping centres, a giant pagoda and those famous highways. It also has a reputation as one of the strangest capitals on Earth. But is it really a ghost town, or does it just look like one from the road? So how do you build a capital the size of a small country, and end up with streets that feel empty?

## Point one · The capital that appeared overnight

For most of its modern history, Myanmar was run from Yangon, a big, crowded port city in the south. That's where the ministries were, the embassies, the business people, the traffic.

And then, on the sixth of November, two thousand and five, the country's administrative capital officially moved. Not to an existing city. To an area in the middle of the country, then known as Pyinmana district, where a new capital had been rising out of the countryside.

Imagine being one of those civil servants. You've lived your whole life in a big, noisy port city. Your family is there, your friends, your favourite tea shop. And then you get the news: your office is moving hundreds of kilometres away, to a city that doesn't have a public name yet.

Government workers were told to move. Ministries packed up files, furniture and staff, and relocated hundreds of kilometres north. For months, the new capital didn't even have a public name. It was only revealed on the twenty-seventh of March, two thousand and six: Naypyidaw.

The official explanation was practical. The government said Yangon was too crowded, and too far from the rest of the country, to work well as a capital. A new city, in the middle, would be easier to reach and easier to run.

## Point two · Built for a future that might come

[GAG: the architect sets up a picnic blanket in the middle lane of a twenty-lane boulevard]
Twenty lanes. Plenty of room for a picnic. Lane fourteen is lovely this time of year.

Now look at the scale. The territory of Naypyidaw covers about seven thousand square kilometres. That's more than four times the size of London. And inside it, everything is spread out on purpose.

The city was built in zones. A zone for ministries. A zone for hotels. A zone for housing. A zone for shopping. Between them, wide, smooth roads that connect one zone to the next. Some of the main boulevards are up to twenty lanes wide.

That kind of design has a logic. If you think a city will grow enormously, you build the roads first, wide enough for the traffic of the future. It's much harder to widen a road later, once buildings line both sides. So you plan big, and you wait for the city to fill in.

And you have to admit, from above, it's impressive. Straight boulevards running to the horizon. Big roundabouts with fountains in the middle. Long, low government buildings set back behind lawns. Everything looks new, tidy and deliberate. It's what a capital looks like when someone gets to design it all at once, on empty land, with no old streets in the way.

There's also a giant golden pagoda, the Uppatasanti Pagoda, completed in two thousand and nine. It's built to be similar in size and shape to the most famous pagoda in Yangon. A new capital, with a new landmark, to match the old one.

Think about what that says. A capital is more than a place where laws are written. It's a symbol. It's where people come to see the country at its most official and most proud. Building a new one from scratch means building new symbols too, and hoping people will come to care about them.

## Rehook

For the people who designed it, the empty land was an advantage. No old buildings to knock down. No narrow streets to fight with. No neighbourhoods to move. Just space, and a chance to put everything exactly where the plan said it should go.

And that's where the strange photos come from. Because a road built for the traffic of the future looks very different on a normal Tuesday in the present.

## Point three · The twenty-lane road

[GAG: the architect waits at a pedestrian crossing; the light turns green; one motorbike passes in the distance]
Twenty lanes, and the rush-hour traffic jam is one motorbike.

Picture standing at the edge of one of those boulevards. Ten lanes going one way, ten lanes going the other. Perfect asphalt. Neat lines. Flowers in the middle. And then you look left, and right, and there's a motorbike far in the distance. Maybe a car. Maybe a worker sweeping the side of the road.

That's the image that went around the world. Visitors in the years after the move found huge, smooth highways with very little traffic. People started calling the new capital a white elephant in the jungle.

## Point four · Empty road, busy office

Here's the twist that most viral videos skip. An empty road doesn't prove an empty city.

Think about how a road gets busy. Traffic jams happen when lots of people need to go to the same place at the same time, on roads that are too small for them. Make the road twenty lanes wide, spread the destinations far apart, and the same number of cars just disappears into all that space. A road can look empty because it's quiet, or because it's simply huge, far bigger than the traffic it carries.

Try a simple experiment in your head. Take the traffic from a normal two-lane street at rush hour, and spread it across twenty lanes. Each lane now gets a tenth of the cars. A busy street becomes a calm one, without a single person leaving. Now spread the buildings out too, so every destination is kilometres apart. The cars don't bunch up anywhere. They just glide.

And people do live and work there. But the same visitors also found ministries full of staff, hotels with guests, shops and restaurants where people went about their day. Around nine hundred thousand people live in the whole territory. That's not a ghost town. But it's spread across an area more than four times the size of London.

So when you see a drone shot of an empty highway, you're seeing something real, but you're not seeing the whole story. You're seeing what happens when a city is planned around distance.

And the camera plays its own trick. A drone shot shows a huge road from above, at one moment, often at a quiet time of day. It can't show the office next to the road, full of people at their desks. It can't show the bus that passed ten minutes ago. It shows space, and space is what this city has more of than almost anywhere else.

## Point five · A day in the life

[GAG: Ko cycles across empty boulevards for ages to reach a sandwich stand]
Lunch break: ten minutes eating, forty minutes travelling to the sandwich.

Let's follow an imaginary civil servant through a normal day. Call him Ko. He lives in a government housing block in the residential zone. Neat buildings, identical rows, quiet streets.

In the morning, Ko gets on a bus or a motorbike to the ministry zone. It's not a short trip. Everything is separated, so home and work are kilometres apart. The roads are smooth and empty, so at least he never gets stuck in traffic.

At lunch, he eats near the office, because the shopping zone is somewhere else entirely. After work, if he wants to see a film or meet friends, that's another ride to another zone. In Yangon, all of that would have been a few streets away, crammed together in noise and chaos. Here, it's calm and clean, and everything is far apart.

In our story, Ko's family is still in Yangon, and he visits home when he can. He likes the quiet, and he really likes never sitting in traffic. He doesn't love the distance. On a Friday evening, the ride to the bus station feels longer than the whole working week.

## Point six · What a capital needs

Here's what makes a capital feel alive. Government buildings are only the start. What really matters is everything that grows around them over decades. Street food. Markets. Universities. Music venues. Old neighbourhoods. Embassies, journalists, protesters, lobbyists, tourists. People who come because the capital is where things happen.

Many of those things stayed in Yangon. Most foreign embassies remained there for years. The business centre of the country didn't move. A lot of foreign embassies stayed behind. The biggest airport and the busiest port didn't move either. So Naypyidaw got the ministries, but Yangon kept much of the life.

## Rehook

Which leaves a simple question that every planned city has to answer sooner or later. What happens when you build the stage, but the audience stays somewhere else?

## Point seven · The cost of distance

[GAG: a road sweeper finishes lane one, looks at the nineteen lanes left, and sighs]
And somebody still has to sweep all of it.

Nobody outside knows the full cost of building Naypyidaw. Estimates and rumours vary, and the government never published a full figure. But you can see the running costs in every photo.

Every one of those twenty lanes of road still needs to be resurfaced, lit and swept every year. Huge buildings need power, cooling and staff. Every kilometre between zones is a kilometre of road, pipe and cable that has to be maintained. A dense, crowded city shares its infrastructure among lots of people. A spread-out city asks fewer people to pay for much more of it.

And distance costs people, too. Every trip takes longer. Every errand needs a ride. Without a car or a motorbike, simple things, like buying groceries after work, become a small expedition.

That's the real trap of building for the future. If the future arrives, the roads fill up, and the plan looks brilliant. If the future arrives slowly, you're paying for a city several times bigger than the one you actually have.

## Closing

So is Naypyidaw a ghost town? Not really. It's a working capital with ministries, hotels and hundreds of thousands of residents. But it was built for a much bigger city than the one that showed up. The roads are the clearest proof. They're the size of a future that hasn't arrived yet.

Building a capital from scratch is one of the biggest bets a country can make. You can move the ministries in a day. You can't move a city's life the same way.

The ministries made the move. The roads were built. What a plan can't order is a city's habits, its friendships and its reasons to stay. Those take a generation, and sometimes more.

Somewhere right in the middle of Myanmar, there's a twenty-lane road, perfectly smooth, waiting for the traffic it was built for.
