## Hook

North Korea's tallest building is a hotel that has never had a single guest. Construction started in nineteen eighty-seven. One hundred and five floors. More than three thousand rooms. It's been standing over the capital for more than thirty years. And not a single guest has ever spent a night in it.

## Lock-in

A foreign magazine once called it the worst building in the history of mankind. The government it belongs to spent years pretending it wasn't there. And today, the empty hotel has a new job that has nothing to do with sleeping. So how does a country build the tallest thing it owns, and then not know what to do with it?

## Point one · A race nobody else was running

[GAG: a scoreboard reads SOUTH vs NORTH; the architect sticks a giant pyramid on the North side]
Cold War rule number one: whatever the neighbours build, build it taller. Whether anyone sleeps in it is a problem for later.

To understand the hotel, you have to look at the map of the nineteen eighties. On one side of the border was South Korea, which was about to host the Olympic Games in Seoul in nineteen eighty-eight. The world's cameras were going there. And a South Korean construction company had just finished building the tallest hotel in the world, in Singapore.

For North Korea, that was more than a building. It was a scoreboard. Every record the South set was a point on the board, and the North wanted points of its own. If the South could build the tallest hotel in the world for somebody else, the North would build an even taller one for itself.

North Korea had its own big event coming. In June nineteen eighty-nine, Pyongyang would host the World Festival of Youth and Students, thousands of young visitors from friendly countries. It needed something that would make those visitors look up.

So the plan was simple and enormous. A pyramid of concrete, three hundred and thirty meters tall, rising out of the middle of Pyongyang. One hundred and five floors. Over three thousand rooms. Revolving restaurants at the top, where guests could eat dinner while the city slowly turned beneath them.

Work began in nineteen eighty-seven. The deadline was the festival, two years away.

## Point two · The deadline that came and went

[GAG: the lift doors open onto a bare concrete shaft; the architect takes the stairs and arrives at floor 105 gasping]
Lifts not included. Cardio very much included.

You can probably guess what happened. Two years is not a lot of time for the tallest building in your country. The festival came in nineteen eighty-nine, and the hotel was nowhere near ready.

But the crews kept going. The pyramid kept climbing. And by nineteen ninety-two, the structure had reached its full height. From a distance, it looked done. A giant grey triangle on the skyline, visible from almost anywhere in the city.

Up close, it was a shell. No windows. No lifts running guests to their rooms. No carpets, no beds, no lights. Just raw concrete floors, stacked a hundred and five times. A crane was left sitting on the very top.

Picture standing on the hundred and fifth floor back then. No glass between you and the wind. Bare concrete under your boots, rusting steel bars sticking out of the edges, and the whole city spread out below. You're higher than anyone else in the country. And there's nothing up here except dust and that crane.

And then, in nineteen ninety-two, the work stopped.

## Rehook

The reason was money. North Korea's economy couldn't support the project anymore. And for the next sixteen years, the tallest building in the country simply stood there, empty, in the middle of the capital.

## Point three · The building that wasn't there

[GAG: a postcard of the city where the pyramid has been clumsily painted over with a fluffy cloud]
Photoshop, years before Photoshop.

Here's the strangest part of this story. You'd think a government would at least admit the building exists. It's three hundred and thirty meters tall. You can't hide it behind a tree.

But by many accounts, North Korea did its best. Official images of the Pyongyang skyline would often leave it out. The hotel was rarely mentioned, and it was frequently airbrushed out of photographs. The country's biggest building became something you weren't supposed to see.

Imagine living in that city. Every day, you walk to work, and there it is above the rooftops, a grey pyramid with empty window holes. Then you open a postcard of your own city, and the skyline is missing its biggest piece. Everyone knows it's there. Nobody talks about it.

A building the size of a mountain had become the most awkward subject in the city. It was too big to finish, too big to knock down, and too big to hide. So it just stayed, year after year, slowly weathering in the open air.

Outside the country, it went the other way. Foreign writers loved it. They called it the Hotel of Doom. And in two thousand and eight, Esquire magazine called it the worst building in the history of mankind.

## Point four · Glass on a ghost

That same year, two thousand and eight, something changed. Workers came back.

This time, there was foreign help. An Egyptian company called Orascom, which was also building North Korea's mobile phone network, got involved in the project. In two thousand and nine, one of its executives said the tower wouldn't be just a hotel. It would be a mixed-use development, with other kinds of space inside as well. For the first time in sixteen years, there was a plan, a partner and money moving.

Officials said the hotel would be finished by twenty twelve, for the hundredth anniversary of the birth of the country's founder, Kim Il-sung. This time, the plan started with the outside. Crews began covering the grey concrete with glass and metal panels, floor after floor, side after side.

By around twenty eleven, the makeover was done. The ugly grey pyramid had become a shining glass one. Suddenly, the Hotel of Doom looked like a real, finished, modern skyscraper. It caught the sunlight. It reflected the clouds.

But think about what glass actually does. It covers things. Behind those shiny panels, the building still needed what every hotel needs. Lifts, plumbing, power, air conditioning, kitchens, furniture and staff. None of that shows up in a photo of the outside.

And do the maths on three thousand rooms. That's three thousand bathrooms. Three thousand sets of pipes that have to reach the top of a three-hundred-meter pyramid. Lifts that can carry guests and luggage up a hundred floors, fast enough that nobody gives up and takes the stairs. Fire systems, kitchens big enough to feed thousands of people, laundry for thousands of beds. Every one of those things costs money and needs parts, and many of those parts have to come from abroad.

Glass makes a building look finished. Pipes, wires and lifts make it actually work, and they're the expensive part that nobody photographs.

## Point five · The hotel company that changed its mind

[GAG: a hotel front desk; the architect rings the bell and dust falls from the ceiling]
"Good evening, checking in." "Wonderful. You're our first guest in twenty-five years." "Which floor?" "Any of them. Honestly, any of them."

In late twenty twelve, it looked like the moment had finally come. Kempinski, a European luxury hotel company, said it would run the hotel. Not all of it, at least not at first. The plan was to open part of it in the middle of twenty thirteen, starting with one hundred and fifty suites on the top floors. Later, about fifteen hundred rooms.

For a building that had waited twenty-five years for its first guest, this was huge news. A big international name was attaching itself to the Hotel of Doom. If they were coming, the inside had to be on its way. Travel writers started imagining the view from the top floors. You could almost picture the first room key.

Think about what that first night would have meant. A guest checking in at the front desk of a building that had been empty for a quarter of a century. Riding a lift past a hundred dark floors to a suite at the very top. Looking out over a capital that, for years, had pretended this building wasn't there. It would have been one of the strangest hotel stays on the planet.

And then, in March twenty thirteen, the plans were suspended. Kempinski said that market entry was not currently possible. Later, the company said it had only ever held talks, and had never signed on to run the hotel at all. It never gave a full public explanation.

So the shining glass pyramid went back to waiting.

Imagine being the person who'd been promised that first room key. A real company, real dates, real suites on the top floor. And then, in a few months, all of it quietly disappears, and the building is exactly what it was before. Tall, shiny and empty.

## Rehook

At this point, most countries might have given up on the idea of a hotel. North Korea found a different use for the building. It didn't need guests. It needed an audience.

## Point six · The biggest screen in the country

[GAG: the architect watches the light show from a deckchair with a bucket of popcorn]
It finally found its calling. Not a hotel. The biggest TV in the country.

In twenty eighteen, one side of the hotel was fitted with a giant screen made of lights, built into the facade. And at night, the empty hotel started to glow.

That December, animations started playing across the building, a light show about four minutes long. Pictures and political slogans, hundreds of meters tall, visible across the whole capital.

Think about the irony. For years, the government tried to keep this building out of pictures. Now it's the biggest picture in the city. The hotel that couldn't host a single guest found a way to host a message instead.

And as a screen, it works perfectly. A screen doesn't need plumbing. It doesn't need staff, or kitchens, or lifts. It only needs power at night and a city full of people who can't help looking up. For the first time in its life, the building is doing exactly what it was built to do: be seen.

Inside, as far as anyone outside can tell, it is still not a working hotel. Nobody has published a check-in date. No one has posted a photo from their room. As far as anyone knows, it has never opened its doors.

## Closing

Look at the pyramid one more time. The tower was always more about the skyline than the hotel. It was started to win a race, timed for a festival, and judged from far away. From far away, it looks finished. Up close, a hotel is thousands of small things that all have to work at once, and none of them can be seen from the street.

The tallest building in North Korea ended up proving the opposite of what it was built to prove. It was meant to show that the country could do anything the South could do, and more. Instead, for decades, it showed the gap between a promise and a working building.

The concrete took five years. The glass took three. The rest, more than thirty-five years after the first shovel went into the ground, still hasn't happened.

Somewhere in Pyongyang, there's a pyramid with more than three thousand rooms, a light show on the outside, and not a single bed that anyone has ever slept in.
