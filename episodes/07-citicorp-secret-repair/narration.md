## Hook

In nineteen seventy-eight, a brand new New York skyscraper could have been knocked over by a storm, and only a handful of people knew. By its own engineer's estimate, a strong enough storm could knock it over. So, while thousands of people worked inside it every day, welders quietly fixed it, joint by joint. And the public didn't hear about it for almost twenty years.

## Lock-in

This is the story of the Citicorp Center, a fifty-nine-storey tower in Manhattan, and the secret repair that kept it standing. It's a story about a bank, a church, a cost-saving change nobody double-checked, and a hurricane heading toward the city at the worst possible moment. And it has a twist that most people who tell it leave out. So how does a brand new skyscraper end up one storm away from disaster?

## Point one · The church that wouldn't move

[GAG: a banker and a priest shake hands over a model of the block]
"We'd like your corner." "You can have the sky above it." "Deal."

The bank wanted to build its headquarters on a block in Midtown Manhattan. There was just one problem. On the corner of that block stood a church, Saint Peter's. And the church had a condition. If the tower was going to go up, there had to be a new church on the same corner. Not connected to the tower. No columns going through it.

So the engineer, William LeMessurier, came up with an unusual solution. Most towers stand on columns at their corners. This one would stand on four giant stilts placed in the middle of each side, so one corner of the tower could float right above the church.

Picture it from the street. A sleek tower, more than fifty storeys tall, and at the bottom, one of its corners just hangs in the air, high above a small church. It looks impossible. That was part of the appeal.

For the bank, it was a statement. A new headquarters that looked like nothing else on the skyline, with a sharply slanted roof at the top and a hollow space at the bottom where a normal building would have solid corners. For the church, it was a promise kept. For the engineer, it was the most interesting puzzle of his career.

To make it work, the weight had to be carried down through a system of diagonal steel braces, stacked up the building in big chevron shapes, and passed into those four stilts. And on top, there was something new for an office tower: a huge concrete block that could slide around on oil, called a tuned mass damper. Its job was to stop the building from swaying too much in the wind.

The tower was dedicated in October nineteen seventy-seven. At fifty-nine storeys, it was one of the tallest buildings in the world.

## Point two · The change nobody told him about

[GAG: two contractors high-five over a cheaper box of bolts while the engineer naps at his desk]
Saving money: always popular. Telling the engineer: optional, apparently.

Here's where the problem started. In the design, the joints in those diagonal braces were supposed to be welded. Welding is strong, but it's slow and it's expensive.

So during construction, the steel company proposed bolting the joints instead. Bolts are cheaper and faster. And LeMessurier's own office accepted the change. But he didn't know.

That kind of thing happens all the time on big projects. Somebody sees a way to save money, the numbers look fine for the loads they're checking, and the change gets approved. Nobody thinks it's dangerous. It's paperwork.

And the change made sense for the case everyone was checking. The building codes of the time asked engineers to check wind hitting a building straight on. For winds like that, the bolted joints were strong enough. The question nobody asked was what happened when the wind came from a different direction, on a building that stood on stilts in the middle of its sides.

And for almost a year, the tower stood there, full of office workers, with bolts where its engineer believed there were welds.

## Rehook

Then, in the summer of nineteen seventy-eight, two things happened at almost the same time. And together, they made LeMessurier look at his own building again.

## Point three · Two questions

The first came from a completely different job. In May, a contractor on another project asked about the cost of welding. LeMessurier pointed them to Citicorp as an example. And that's when he found out that Citicorp's joints weren't welded at all.

The second came from a student. In June, Diane Hartley, an engineering student at Princeton, was writing her senior thesis about the building's design, at the suggestion of her professor. She had questions about how the tower handled wind. Especially wind hitting it not straight on the face, but at an angle, from the corners.

Those are called quartering winds. This particular tower had a weak spot. Because of how the stilts and braces worked, a wind coming diagonally loaded the structure in a different way.

When LeMessurier went back to his calculations, the numbers were alarming. A diagonal wind increased the force on some of the braces by about forty percent. And on the bolted joints, the increase came out at about one hundred and sixty percent.

Think about what that means. The joints that were supposed to be the strongest part of the frame were now its weakest. And they were the ones that had been changed to save money. A forty percent extra push from the wind turned into more than double the strain on the bolts, because of how the forces were funneled through the braces.

## Point four · The weekend in Maine

[GAG: the architect in a holiday cabin buried in crumpled paper; the calculator screen glows]
Nothing ruins a summer holiday like finding out your skyscraper might fall over.

At the end of July, LeMessurier went to his summer house in Maine and worked through the problem alone. And the answer he reached was frightening.

By his estimate, with the tuned mass damper working, a wind strong enough to topple the tower would hit New York on average about once every fifty-five years. That's already not great for a building meant to last a century.

But the damper needed electricity. And in a big storm, the power can go out. Without the damper, his estimate dropped to a storm strong enough to topple the building about once every sixteen years.

Now imagine being in his position. You've just realised that a famous building you designed might come down in a storm, in the middle of Manhattan. You could stay quiet and hope. Or you could tell your client, the bank, its lawyers and the city, and put your own reputation and career on the line.

He chose to tell them. He went to the architect, to his insurers, to the bank's top people, and laid out the problem. It was the kind of conversation most professionals spend their careers trying to avoid. Here's the building I designed. Here's why it might fall. Here's what we need to do, starting now.

## Point five · Fixing it in secret

What happened next moved very fast. The bank agreed to the repair. The plan was simple to describe and hard to carry out. Over two hundred bolted joints would be reinforced by welding thick steel plates, two inches of steel, over them.

The work went on around the clock, for about three months. Welders went in behind the office walls and ceilings, working on joints hidden inside the building. During the day, thousands of people came to work as usual, sat at their desks and went home. They had no idea that a few meters away, inside the walls, crews were reinforcing the skeleton of the building they were sitting in.

The damper was given backup power, so a blackout wouldn't switch it off. And plans were prepared for what to do if the worst happened, including how to clear the streets around the tower if a dangerous storm came before the work was done.

Picture being one of those welders. You're cutting into the walls of an office tower in New York, fixing the joints of a building that could fall, and you can't tell anyone why.

[GAG: an office worker hears welding behind the wall and turns his radio up]
If you heard welding inside the walls at work, would you ask? Nobody did.

## Point six · The hurricane

[GAG: the architect stares at a weather map, blows on the hurricane icon, and it drifts out to sea]
Engineering is ninety-nine percent maths and one percent luck.

And then, in the middle of the repair, the weather turned against them. Hurricane Ella was moving up the Atlantic, and New York was in its possible path.

This was the exact scenario that kept LeMessurier awake. A strong storm, with the work not finished. Everyone involved watched the forecasts. Every update on the radio mattered. Every change in the hurricane's track changed the odds for a tower full of people who didn't know there was anything to worry about.

And then Ella turned. It veered east and went out to sea. The storm never reached the city. The welders kept working, and the repair was finished.

## Rehook

The tower was safe. The story stayed quiet. For almost two decades, the people who walked past it every day had no idea how close it had come.

## Point seven · The story comes out

The secret held until nineteen ninety-five, when the whole story finally came out in print. And it became a legend in engineering schools. An engineer who admitted his own mistake, a hidden repair, a hurricane that turned away at the last moment. It's taught as a lesson in ethics: when you find a problem, you speak up, even when it costs you.

And there's a reason the story spread so well. It has everything. A hidden flaw. A young student asking a sharp question. A lone engineer doing the maths in a summer house. A race against a hurricane. And a happy ending, where the building is saved and nobody gets hurt.

## Point eight · The twist

But here's the part that's usually left out. Years later, engineers went back to the case. They used wind measurements from another building with similar proportions, and they ran the numbers again.

Their analysis suggested that winds hitting the flat faces of the building could have mattered as much as the corner winds, or even more. And they were careful about it. They said their conclusion had limits, because some of the original measurements simply aren't available anymore.

So the famous version, the diagonal wind that only a student spotted, might not be the whole story. The building really did need fixing. But the exact reason it needed fixing is more complicated than the legend.

## Closing

It all came down to one small change. A small, sensible-looking change on a very unusual building. Bolts instead of welds. Nobody checked how that change behaved in every kind of wind, on a tower standing on stilts above a church.

And what went right? People acted on the data they had, before they had all of it. They didn't wait for a perfect answer. They fixed the building first.

Today, the tower still stands in Midtown, with its corner floating above the church. Most of the people inside it have never heard about the plates welded into its walls. And maybe that's the best ending a repair can have: done so well that nobody ever noticed.
