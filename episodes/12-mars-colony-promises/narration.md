## Lock-in

This is the story of the Mars colony that keeps moving. Not on the map. On the calendar. Two very different companies, one tiny and one giant, both promised that people would soon be living on another planet. One of them went bankrupt. The other is still building rockets. So why is getting to Mars always a few years away?

## Point one · One-way ticket

[GAG: a job interview; the architect reads a form to a nervous candidate]
"Why do you want to go to Mars?" "Adventure. Science. History." "Great. It's one way." "Sorry, what?"

Let's start with the small one. In the early twenty tens, a Dutch company called Mars One announced a plan that sounded like science fiction. It would put a permanent human settlement on Mars. The first people would land by twenty twenty-three.

There was a catch. The trip was one-way. Nobody was coming back. Not after a year, not after ten. The settlers would live, work and eventually die on Mars.

And how would the company pay for it? Partly with television. The plan was to turn the selection and training of the astronauts into a reality show, and use the money from the global audience to fund the missions.

Then came the applications. Mars One said more than two hundred thousand people expressed interest in going. That number went around the world. Two hundred thousand people ready to leave Earth forever.

Think about what kind of story that is. A one-way trip. A new world. A global audience watching ordinary people compete for the chance to go. It had everything a headline needs. What it didn't have, at least not yet, was a single piece of hardware.

## Point two · The number behind the number

Here's the detail that most headlines missed. Expressing interest isn't the same as applying. Later, it came out that only two thousand seven hundred and sixty-one people actually completed the application. Mars One later confirmed that figure.

That's still a lot of people. But it's about one percent of the number in the headlines.

And the company had a much bigger problem than paperwork. It didn't build rockets. It didn't build landers. It didn't build habitats. All of that would have to be bought from someone else, at a price nobody had ever paid, for a mission nobody had ever flown.

Picture being one of the candidates. You've told your family you might leave Earth forever. You've done interviews. Your name is on a shortlist. And meanwhile, nobody can show you the spacecraft that's supposed to take you there, because it doesn't exist.

## Point three · The end of Mars One

The dates started slipping, pushed back again and again. Every new announcement put the first landing a little further away.

And the money was always the problem. Television money and donations are one thing. A mission to Mars costs the kind of money only governments and the very biggest companies can spend. Mars One was trying to buy the most expensive trip in history with the budget of a small media company.

On the fifteenth of January, twenty nineteen, a court in Basel, Switzerland declared the company's business arm, Mars One Ventures, bankrupt. The settlement that was supposed to have people on Mars by twenty twenty-three ended four years earlier, in a courtroom on Earth.

For the candidates, the news must have landed hard. For years, you've been one of the people who might go to Mars. Now there's no company, no mission and no ship. The only thing left is the story you tell at dinner parties.

Mars One was small. It never had the engineering behind its promises. But the giant in this story was very different.

The Mars mission ended four years early. On Earth. In Switzerland.

## Rehook

Because at the same time, a company that really did build rockets was making its own Mars promises. And its plan was much, much bigger.

## Point four · A city of a million

On the twenty-seventh of September, twenty sixteen, at a space conference in Mexico, Elon Musk presented SpaceX's plan for Mars. A giant rocket and a spaceship that could carry at least a hundred people per trip.

The goal wasn't a small base. It was a city. A self-sustaining city of around a million people. He estimated it could take somewhere between forty and a hundred years to build. And he said he hoped people could start flying to Mars in about ten years.

Picture the room. A big conference hall full of engineers, scientists and journalists from around the world. On the screen, animations of a giant rocket lifting off, a spaceship refuelling in orbit, and a fleet of ships heading to a red planet together. People were filming the screen with their phones.

That's the kind of vision that grabs the whole world. Not a flag and some footprints. A civilisation, on another planet, with thousands of ships flying back and forth.

A year later, in twenty seventeen, a new version of the plan came with dates. At least two cargo ships to Mars in twenty twenty-two. Then, in twenty twenty-four, two more cargo ships and two ships with crews.

## Point five · The calendar problem

[GAG: the architect sprints for a departing Mars train and misses it; the board flips to NEXT DEPARTURE: 26 MONTHS]
Missed the bus to Mars? No problem. The next one's in two years.

Here's something that makes Mars different from the Moon. You can't just go whenever you're ready.

Earth and Mars both circle the Sun, at different speeds. The best moments to travel between them come around only about once every twenty-six months, when the two planets line up in the right way. Miss that window, and you wait more than two years for the next one.

So every Mars plan is really a list of windows. Twenty twenty-two. Twenty twenty-four. Twenty twenty-six. Twenty twenty-eight. Each window is a deadline you either meet or miss, and the sky doesn't negotiate. And a miss doesn't cost you weeks. It costs you two years.

A mission planner lives by this rule, every single day of the job. You can't just say we'll launch when it's ready. Ready has to line up with the sky. If your ship is one month late for the window, it's not one month late. It's twenty-six months late.

Now look at the twenty seventeen plan through that lens. Cargo in twenty twenty-two meant a working ship, tested and ready, about five years after the presentation. Crews in twenty twenty-four meant a ship that could keep people alive for months, land them safely, and support them on the surface.

## Point six · What happened instead

[GAG: a departure board where every date keeps flipping to a later year]
Mars: always a few years away. Every year.

Twenty twenty-two came, and no ship went to Mars. Twenty twenty-four came, and no crew went either.

That doesn't mean nothing happened. SpaceX kept building and testing its giant rocket, now called Starship. Some test flights ended in huge explosions. Others went further than before. Each one taught the engineers something. But testing a rocket around Earth is a very different thing from landing people on another planet.

And to be fair, building the biggest rocket in history is genuinely hard. Every test flight is an experiment. When something goes wrong, the engineers find out why, change the design, and try again. That's how rockets have always been built. It's just that this way of building doesn't fit neatly into dates announced years in advance.

In May twenty twenty-five, Musk gave a fifty-fifty chance that an uncrewed Starship would launch toward Mars by the end of twenty twenty-six. Later, he said twenty twenty-eight was more likely. And by twenty twenty-six, the attempt for that window had been quietly shelved. One big reason: a key step, refuelling a ship in orbit, still hadn't been demonstrated.

So the next realistic window is around the end of twenty twenty-eight. More than a decade after the plan with the twenty twenty-four date on it.

## Rehook

So is this a collapse? Mars One clearly collapsed. SpaceX is still flying. The honest answer is that the dates collapsed, again and again, while the project kept going.

## Point seven · Why Mars dates slip

Getting to Mars isn't one problem. It's a chain of problems, and every link has to work.

First, you need a rocket powerful enough to lift a huge ship. Then you need to refuel it in orbit, because it can't carry enough fuel for the whole trip from the ground. Then the ship has to survive months in deep space, land on a planet with a thin atmosphere, and, if there are people on board, keep them alive the whole time. Then, if you want a settlement, you need power, water, air and food on a planet that offers none of them for free.

Picture the most boring thing that could go wrong. A fridge breaks. On Earth, you call a repair shop. On Mars, the nearest spare part is months away, and the next delivery might be two years out. Now multiply that by every pump, filter, seal and battery a settlement needs. Air has to be made. Water has to be found or recycled. Food has to be grown or shipped. A settlement that survives only between deliveries isn't a city yet. It's an outpost on a very long supply line.

## Point eight · What to watch for

So how do you tell a real step from a promise? Here's a simple checklist. Has a ship refuelled in orbit? Has an uncrewed ship landed on Mars in one piece? Has cargo arrived and been used on the surface? Have people landed and come home, or stayed and survived until the next delivery?

Every one of those is a milestone you can see, with photos and data. Until they happen, a date is just a date.

And there's a lesson in that for every megaproject, not just Mars. A big presentation can show you a destination. Only hardware can show you progress. When you hear a date, the useful question isn't whether it sounds exciting. It's what has to work first, and whether that has been shown yet.

## Closing

Mars itself never let anyone down. The dream of people living on Mars is still alive, and real engineering is chasing it. What went wrong was the calendar. Mars One sold a date it had no way to reach, and went bankrupt. SpaceX set dates far ahead of its hardware, and watched them pass one window at a time.

Maybe one day, a ship really will land there with people on board. If it does, it will be one of the greatest achievements in human history. But it will happen on the day the hardware is ready, not on the day the slide says.

Every twenty-six months, Earth and Mars line up again. And every time they do, there's a new date on the board.
