## Hook

People have dreamed of a canal across Nicaragua since the fifteen hundreds. In twenty thirteen, the country finally gave a Chinese businessman the right to build one, across the entire country. Bigger than the Panama Canal. Right through the largest lake in Central America. The first cost estimate was fifty billion dollars. A groundbreaking ceremony was held in twenty fourteen. And then, for almost ten years, nobody dug the canal.

## Lock-in

This is the story of a canal that only ever existed on paper, and of the paper itself. Because in this case, the most important object isn't a ship, a dredger or a dam. It's a legal document. It was signed in twenty thirteen, and it was cancelled in twenty twenty-four. In between, there was a ceremony, a lot of fear, and very little digging. So how can a canal be approved, celebrated and cancelled, without ever really being started?

## Point one · The dream of a second canal

[GAG: a Spanish surveyor in 1551 holds a ruler across a map of Nicaragua; a modern surveyor holds the exact same ruler]
Nearly five hundred years, same ruler, same idea.

For more than a century, ships travelling between the Atlantic and the Pacific have had one shortcut through the Americas: the Panama Canal, which opened in nineteen fourteen. Every ship that doesn't fit, or doesn't want to wait, has to go the long way round, thousands of kilometres around the bottom of South America.

And for even longer, people have looked at the map of Nicaragua and seen a second route. Spanish colonial surveyors were already studying it in fifteen fifty-one. Nicaragua has a huge lake in the middle, Lake Nicaragua, and it's not far from either coast. On a map, it looks like nature already did half the work.

The idea is old. Planners and explorers looked at this route long before the Panama Canal was dug. Each time, it looked tempting on paper, and each time, someone else's canal, or someone else's money, got there first.

So in twenty thirteen, the government did something bold. On the thirteenth of June, it approved a concession for a company called HKND Group, owned by a Chinese businessman named Wang Jing. The concession gave the company the sole right to plan and build a canal, and then to run it, for decades.

The plan was enormous. A route of about two hundred and seventy kilometres, from the Pacific coast to the Caribbean, cutting straight through the lake. Ports, a free trade zone, an airport, roads and resorts. The first estimate was around fifty billion dollars.

To put that in perspective, that was several times the size of Nicaragua's entire economy at the time. A country of a few million people was being promised the biggest construction project in its history, paid for by a private company from the other side of the world.

## Point two · What a concession actually is

[GAG: the architect hangs a framed permit on a fence post in an empty field]
A permit to build a canal. Lovely frame. No canal.

Here's the part most headlines got wrong. A concession isn't a canal. It isn't even money.

Think of it like a building permit. A permit says you're allowed to build a house on a piece of land. It doesn't pay for the bricks. It doesn't hire the workers. It doesn't guarantee that anyone will ever live in the house. It's permission, and nothing more. You can frame it and hang it on the wall. You still can't live in it.

The Nicaragua concession was a very big permission, probably one of the biggest a small country has ever handed to a single private company. It allowed one company to try to build one of the biggest engineering projects on Earth. But to actually do it, that company needed fifty billion dollars or more, engineers, contractors, years of studies, and customers willing to pay to use the canal once it was finished.

The permission was signed. Everything else still had to be found.

## Point three · The groundbreaking

In December twenty fourteen, there was a groundbreaking ceremony. Officials, speeches, cameras, the symbolic first moment of construction.

Picture the scene. A dusty piece of land on the Pacific side. A stage. Flags. People in hard hats who will probably never wear them again. A ceremonial start for a canal designed to carry some of the largest ships in the world.

And then, nothing much happened. The ceremony took place. Real construction of the canal didn't follow. No fleets of dredgers appeared on the lake. No locks rose on the coasts. The site of the ceremony went quiet.

Imagine being someone who lived along the planned route. The government has announced a canal through your region. Maybe through your village, your farm, the land your family has worked for generations. There's a ceremony on the news. And then, year after year, no canal arrives, but the plan doesn't go away either.

## Rehook

And that uncertainty hurt real people. Because a canal that's never built can still change the lives of the people living in its path.

## Point four · The fear along the route

A route two hundred and seventy kilometres long passes through a lot of land, and a lot of homes. Critics warned that rural communities could be displaced. Farmers along the route protested. They wanted to know: would their land be taken? Would they be paid? Where would they go?

And then there was the lake. Lake Nicaragua is the country's largest reserve of fresh water. A canal for giant ships would mean dredging it, and changing it, in ways that scientists and critics said could put that water at risk. It's a question that matters for millions of people: what happens to a country's drinking water when you turn its biggest lake into a shipping lane?

Put yourself in a farmer's place. You don't know if your land is on the final route. You don't know if you'll be paid, or how much, or when. Do you plant this year's crop? Do you repair the barn? Do you sell now, before the price drops? A plan that hangs over you for years can freeze your life even if no digger ever arrives.

The plan never got far enough to answer those questions in the real world. But the questions themselves were enough to make people afraid.

## Point five · The money that wasn't there

[GAG: a shipping boss and a canal boss sit across a table, both with arms folded]
"We'll use your canal once it's built." "We'll build it once you use it."

So where was the money? The canal needed tens of billions of dollars, and it needed them for years, long before a single ship paid a toll. And the businessman behind it had a very bad few years.

Wang Jing's fortune was estimated to have peaked at around six point nine billion dollars in twenty fifteen. Within a few years, estimates said it had fallen below one billion. That's a lot of money for a person. It's nowhere near enough for a canal that was supposed to cost fifty billion.

And without big investors, banks and shipping companies committing to the project, a concession is just a piece of paper with a lot of promises on it.

Imagine you're a shipping company. Someone shows you a map with a new canal on it. Will you sign a contract to use it? Only if you believe it will be finished, safe and reliable. And you won't believe that until you see digging, dredging and concrete. But the people building it need your commitment before they can raise the money to start. Everyone is waiting for everyone else.

## Point six · The paper ends

For almost ten years, the concession sat there. It was still law. The company still had its rights. But nothing was being built.

Then, on the eighth of May, twenty twenty-four, Nicaragua's National Assembly repealed the law that had granted the concession to HKND. The change was published in the country's official gazette. The permission that had started the whole story was cancelled.

There was no closing ceremony this time. No stage, no flags, no speeches about the future. Just a vote, and a few pages in an official publication. The canal ended the way it had begun: on paper.

Here's the important detail. The repeal ended that specific concession, the one given to that one company. It didn't erase the idea of a canal through Nicaragua. A broader law calling for a canal stayed on the books. But who would ever pay for it was, once again, an open question.

## Rehook

So the canal was never built, and the permission is gone. But this story has a strange mirror image, and you've seen it on this channel before.

## Point seven · The opposite of Dubai

Remember the Dubai islands from the first episode? There, the land was real. The sand was dredged, the islands were built, every one of them is still out there. What was missing was everything that makes land useful: pipes, power, bridges and people.

Nicaragua is the exact opposite. The paperwork was real. The permission was signed, the route was drawn, a ceremony was held. What was missing was the physical thing itself. The dredgers, the locks, the ports, the money.

One project built the ground and couldn't connect it. The other drew the connection and never built the ground. Both show the same thing from different sides: a megaproject is only real when every part of it exists at the same time. The sand, the pipes, the money, the permission and the people. Take any of them away, and you're left with an island nobody lives on, or a canal nobody digs.

## Point eight · Drawing a line through a lake

It's easy to draw a canal on a map. A line from one coast to the other, through a lake, done. It looks simple, almost obvious.

Try it yourself. Take a ruler, put it on a map of Central America, and draw from coast to coast through the lake. It takes a few seconds. The real thing would take years of dredging, blasting, building locks and moving earth, through jungle, farmland and water.

Running a working waterway is something else. Locks that lift ships up and down. Water that has to be managed every single day. Pilots to guide the ships, and crews to keep everything running. Customers who need to trust that the canal will be open tomorrow. A canal isn't a line. It's a machine that has to work forever.

## Closing

So what was the Nicaragua canal, in the end? A permission without a project. A ceremony without a construction site. A line on a map that made thousands of people afraid for their land and their water, and then disappeared from the law books in twenty twenty-four.

And the people along the route? For them, the end of the concession was mostly the end of a long wait. The threat they had lived with for a decade was lifted by a vote they had no part in, just as it had arrived.

Somewhere in a government archive, there's a gazette from June twenty thirteen that granted the right to cut a country in half. And there's another, from May twenty twenty-four, that took it back. Between those two pieces of paper, the canal stayed exactly where it started: on a map.
