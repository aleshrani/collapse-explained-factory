## Hook

Germany built a gun so big it needed its own railway to move. It weighed more than thirteen hundred tonnes. Each shell weighed about as much as a big truck. And by the time the gun was finally ready, the battle it had been built for was already over.

## Lock-in

This is the story of the biggest gun ever fired in war, and why it might also be one of the worst deals any army ever made. Because the problem with building the biggest anything is that you also have to move it, feed it, protect it and find something worth pointing it at. So how did a gun designed to break one wall end up as a pile of scrap?

## Point one · A wall nobody could break

[GAG: the architect measures the French wall with a tape measure, then unrolls a far, far longer tape for the gun]
The army's answer to a very big wall was a very, very big gun. Thorough. Very thorough.

To understand the gun, you have to understand the wall. After the First World War, France built a line of fortresses along its border with Germany. It was called the Maginot Line. Concrete bunkers, steel turrets, tunnels, underground railways, all designed so that an attacking army would break its teeth on it.

For the French, the line was a promise. Never again would a war be fought across their fields the way the last one had been. For the German army, it was a problem with a very specific shape. Thick concrete, buried deep, and built to take a beating from any gun that existed at the time.

In the mid nineteen thirties, the German army went to the steel company Krupp with a question. Could they build a gun big enough to smash the strongest forts of that line? Not damage them. Smash them, from far enough away that the fort couldn't fire back.

Krupp's engineers did the maths, and the maths gave them a monster. The barrel would need an opening of eighty centimeters. That's wide enough for an adult to crawl inside. The shells would weigh around seven tonnes each. And the whole thing, assembled, would weigh more than thirteen hundred tonnes.

Imagine standing next to one of those shells. It's taller than you, made of solid steel, and it weighs about as much as a loaded truck. Now imagine loading it. Not by hand, obviously. With machinery, one shell at a time, every time.

Now, here's the part that sounds clever at first. A gun that heavy can't drive anywhere. No road could take it. So it would ride on rails, on its own double track, and wherever it needed to go, the railway would go first.

## Point two · Building the impossible

[GAG: a worker asks for a spare part; a clerk points at an empty shelf labelled GUSTAV PARTS]
Spare parts? Of course. Come back in two years.

Building it took years. Every piece was enormous. The barrel alone was a single object longer than most houses are tall. The carriage that held it was so wide it needed two parallel railway tracks under it, side by side, with dozens of wheels spreading the weight.

Picture the factory floor. Cranes the size of buildings. Workers standing next to a barrel and looking like toy soldiers. Every bolt, every gear, every wheel had to be made just for this one machine. There was no spare part on any shelf anywhere in the world.

And here's something people forget about giant projects. The bigger the thing, the fewer people in the world know how to make it. If a measurement is wrong, there's no second supplier to call. If a part cracks, you start again from raw steel. Every problem costs weeks, not days.

And while the engineers were measuring and machining, the calendar didn't wait.

## Rehook

In May nineteen forty, the German army attacked France. And it didn't go through the Maginot Line at all.

## Point three · The wall that didn't matter

[GAG: an officer salutes and points at a map; the arrow goes around the wall instead of through it]
"Sir, the gun is ready." "Excellent. Where's the wall?" "About that. We went around it."

It went around it. Through Belgium, through forests the French had thought were too difficult for a big army. In a few weeks, France had fallen. The fortresses the gun was designed to crack were left behind, mostly untouched.

In other words, the most expensive answer in the history of artillery had arrived at the exam after the exam was over.

Think about that from Krupp's side. You've spent years building the biggest gun in history for one job, and the job is gone. The wall still stands. Nobody needs it broken anymore. And your gun isn't even finished.

The first barrel was test-fired in September nineteen forty-one. That same year, at a test range on the Baltic coast, Alfried Krupp showed the finished gun to Hitler and his generals. Krupp had named it after a member of his own family. Gustav.

And in the tradition of the company, Krupp didn't charge for the first one. The second gun, which the crews called Dora, was not free. That one cost seven million Reichsmarks.

So now there were two of the biggest guns ever made, and the obvious question was: what are they for?

You can't park a thirteen-hundred-tonne gun in a shed and forget it. It needs guards, rail space, maintenance crews and a plan. Every month it sits idle, it costs money and it proves nothing. The army needed a target that could justify the whole thing, and it needed one fast.

## Point four · Moving a monster

[GAG: the architect lays one railway sleeper, wipes his forehead, and looks at miles of empty ground ahead]
Step one, build the gun. Step two, build a railway for the gun. Step three, build more railway for the cranes that build the gun.

The answer was a city on the Black Sea called Sevastopol, in Crimea, which was heavily fortified and under siege in nineteen forty-two. On paper, it was the kind of target the gun had been designed for. Thick walls, forts, ammunition stores dug deep into rock.

But to use the gun, first you had to bring the railway. Here's the job. The gun travels in pieces, on dozens of railway cars. When it gets near the city, workers build a special spur line off the main track. At the end of the spur, they lay four curved tracks, side by side, because the gun can only aim by moving along a curve. The two outer tracks aren't even for the gun. They're for the giant cranes needed to put it back together.

That's not an afternoon of work. Setting up took around five weeks. Thousands of men were involved, laying track, guarding it, running supplies, assembling the pieces. Sources disagree on how many, but the numbers given are in the thousands, all for one gun.

Now picture the view from above. A small town of tents and cranes, with supply wagons parked in long rows, curling around four railway arcs in the countryside. Smoke from the engines. Men carrying sleepers, bolting rails, unloading pieces of steel the size of houses. And in the middle, slowly rising off the ground, the gun.

## Point five · A few dozen shots

On the fifth of June, nineteen forty-two, the gun was ready. And over the siege, it fired only a few dozen times.

Let that sink in. Years of design, a custom factory process, two guns, a railway built just to reach the spot, five weeks of assembly, and thousands of men, for a few dozen shells.

Each one was huge. But a siege isn't won by one shell. It's won by many smaller guns, planes, supplies and soldiers, day after day. So ask the uncomfortable question. What else could all that steel and money have built, and what could those thousands of men have done instead? Hundreds of ordinary guns, or trucks, or rail cars that could carry supplies every single day. The gun did damage, but it's hard to show that it changed the outcome.

And here's the cruel part for the engineers. Every big gun wears its barrel down, shot by shot, from the heat and pressure inside. Most guns can get a new barrel from a warehouse. This one had exactly one maker, one factory and one design. Nothing about it was easy to replace.

## Rehook

So the biggest gun in history had missed its first war and done little in its second. And the war was still going.

## Point six · Waiting on the rails

[GAG: a soldier polishes the giant barrel while calendar pages fly off the wall behind him]
The most expensive object in the army, and the main job was polishing it.

After Sevastopol, the guns were taken apart again. Packed onto their railway cars. Moved away. And then, mostly, they waited.

Here's the problem with a weapon that needs five weeks to set up. By the time you've built the track, the front line has moved. The enemy has planes now, and a gun that sits on a curved track for weeks is a very large, very obvious target. Every plan to use it again ran into the same wall that had nothing to do with concrete. Time.

Imagine you're the officer in charge of it. You have the most famous gun in the army, a crew of hundreds, and a train of special cars. And you spend your days guarding something you can't use.

Every week costs fuel, food, rail capacity and soldiers who could be somewhere else. Every week, someone above you asks when it will be ready to move. And every week, the honest answer is: not yet, and maybe not at all.

## Point seven · How it ended

As the war turned against Germany, the guns became a liability. They were too big to hide and too slow to save. Near the end of the war, Germany's own side wrecked them so the advancing armies couldn't capture them intact.

Accounts of exactly what happened to each piece disagree. Some say one gun was broken up before the end, others say both were blown up in the final months. But the result is the same. Neither gun survived the war. The biggest guns ever fired in combat ended up as scattered, twisted metal near the railway lines that had carried them.

No complete example exists today. You can't visit it in a museum or stand next to its wheels. For a machine that was built to be the biggest of its kind, it left remarkably little behind. All that's left are photos, a few fragments, and a question that military historians still argue about. Was it ever worth it?

## Closing

Here's what went wrong. The gun worked, in the narrowest sense. It could be built, it could be moved, and it could fire. But it was designed for one wall, and the war simply walked around that wall. Everything after that was a giant machine looking for a job.

That's the trap of building the biggest version of anything. The size becomes the point. And the bigger it gets, the more railway, cranes, men and weeks it needs before it can do anything at all.

There's a lesson here that shows up in almost every collapse on this channel. A project gets designed for one moment in time. The moment passes. But by then, so much money, pride and effort has gone in that nobody wants to say stop. So the project keeps going, looking for a new reason to exist.

Krupp gave the first one away for free. In the end, that might have been the most honest price.
