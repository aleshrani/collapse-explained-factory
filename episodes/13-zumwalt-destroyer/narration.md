## Hook

The US Navy planned to build thirty-two of its most advanced warships ever. It built three. By one government measure of total cost, each of those three came out at more than ten billion dollars. And the ammunition for their main guns got so expensive that the Navy cancelled it, leaving brand new ships with guns that had nothing to fire.

## Lock-in

They're called the Zumwalt-class destroyers. Sharp, angular ships that look like they sailed out of a science fiction film. They were supposed to change naval warfare. Instead, they became one of the most famous examples of how a big program can shrink, and shrink, and shrink, until the price of each ship becomes almost impossible to explain. So how did that happen?

## Point one · The ship of the future

[GAG: the architect polishes the angular ship like a sports car]
It looks like a stealth fighter decided to become a boat.

The idea started in the nineteen nineties. The Navy wanted a new kind of ship. One that would be hard to spot on radar, run on an advanced electric power system, and need a much smaller crew than older ships thanks to automation.

The design looked nothing like a normal warship. Instead of the usual cluttered decks and masts, it had smooth, sloping sides and a single angular block for a superstructure. The hull even leaned inward above the waterline, the opposite of most ships.

Picture seeing it for the first time at a dock. A huge, grey, faceted shape, almost like a giant arrowhead lying on the water. No forest of antennas, no stacks of equipment on deck. It looks less like a ship and more like a piece of modern sculpture.

Inside, the ideas were just as new. Instead of separate systems for moving the ship and powering its equipment, an integrated electric system would feed everything from one source. Computers would handle many jobs that sailors used to do by hand. A smaller crew meant lower running costs for decades. On paper, the future was also cheaper.

The lead ship was named after Admiral Elmo Zumwalt, a former head of the Navy known for pushing it to modernise. It was a fitting name for a ship that was supposed to be the future.

And the plan was to build a lot of them. Thirty-two. Enough to become a real part of the fleet, not a curiosity. Enough to spread the enormous cost of inventing the ship across many hulls, so that each one would be affordable.

## Point two · Thirty-two, sixteen, eight, three

Here's where the story starts to turn. New technology is expensive. Every new system on the ship had to be invented, tested and fixed. And as the costs of developing all that technology grew, the Navy kept cutting the number of ships it planned to buy.

First, thirty-two became sixteen. Then, a few years later, sixteen became eight. And in the end, the program was cut to just three ships.

Twenty-nine ships disappeared from the plan. They were never built and scrapped. They were simply never ordered.

The planning documents over those years tell the story. A neat line of thirty-two ship names, with dates for each one. Then a new version, with half of them crossed out. Then another, with half again crossed out. And finally a page with three names on it, and a lot of white space.

Now, here's the trap. Developing a new ship design costs a huge amount of money before the first one is even built. Designs, testing, new systems, software. That cost is the same whether you build thirty-two ships or three.

## Point three · The mould problem

[GAG: in a toy shop, the architect sells a toy boat for three hundred thousand dollars; the customer faints]
Three toys from one mould. Bargain.

Think about it like making a mould for a toy. Designing and building the mould is expensive. Let's say it costs a million dollars. If you make a million toys from it, each toy carries one dollar of mould cost. If you make three toys, each one carries more than three hundred thousand dollars.

That's what happened to the Zumwalts. All the money spent inventing the ship had to be divided among just three hulls.

And every cut made the next cut more likely. Fewer ships meant a higher price per ship. A higher price per ship made the program look worse in budget meetings. Looking worse made it easier to cut again. It's a spiral that's very hard to climb out of once it starts.

So what did each ship really cost? It depends on what you count, and that's important. Counting only the cost of building the hulls, it's around four and a half billion dollars each. Counting everything the Government Accountability Office includes in total acquisition cost, including all that development, it comes to about ten point six billion dollars per ship. That's more than seven times the original estimate for each ship. The whole program, research included, is around thirty-two billion dollars.

Somebody has to explain those numbers in public, in front of people who want one simple answer. Every one of them is true. They just answer different questions.

And that's a big lesson for reading any headline about a megaproject. When you see a scary number, ask what it's divided by. Is it the cost to build one more? The cost including all the research? The cost over the whole life of the program? The same project can sound reasonable or absurd, depending on which slice you pick.

## Rehook

And then there was the gun. Because the most famous problem with the Zumwalts isn't the hull. It's what was supposed to be fired from it.

## Point four · The gun with no shells

[GAG: an officer stands by the huge gun turret holding an empty shell crate]
"The gun is ready, sir." "Great. Load it." "With what, exactly?"

The ships were designed around a powerful new gun system, with ammunition that could reach targets far away. Each ship carried two of them. On paper, it was one of the reasons the ships existed.

But the special ammunition was designed for a fleet of thirty-two ships, buying lots of rounds. When the fleet shrank to three, the number of rounds the Navy would buy shrank too. And the price of each round went up.

Projections put the cost at up to around eight hundred thousand dollars per round. Close to the price of a small missile, for a single shell.

In twenty sixteen, the Navy dropped it. And the guns, the big reason these ships had been designed the way they were, were left largely unused. Two expensive guns on each of three expensive ships, with no ammunition to fire.

Imagine being a sailor on one of those ships. You serve on the most advanced destroyer in the fleet. You walk past the big gun turrets every day. And everyone on board knows they're mostly for show. It's like owning a sports car with no fuel that fits the engine.

## Point five · The breakdown

Even the first big moment for the lead ship had a twist. The USS Zumwalt was commissioned in October twenty sixteen, in a big ceremony.

Only weeks later, while it was travelling to its new home port on the Pacific, it broke down in the Panama Canal and had to be towed.

That kind of thing can happen to any new ship. New ships have problems, and crews find and fix them. But when your ship is famous for being expensive and futuristic, a breakdown in one of the busiest waterways in the world becomes a headline.

[GAG: the futuristic destroyer is towed through the canal by a tiny tugboat while tourists wave]
The most advanced destroyer in the world, towed like a car with a flat tyre.

## Point six · Small fleets, strange problems

Here's a problem nobody thinks about when a program shrinks. A fleet of three ships is an awkward thing to own.

Spare parts are made in tiny numbers, so they're expensive. Training is designed for a handful of crews. Every system is unique to these three ships, so nothing can be borrowed from the rest of the Navy. Picture trying to keep three one-of-a-kind cars running for thirty years, where every replacement part has to be custom-made. That's what owning a tiny fleet feels like.

And when one ship is in the shipyard for work, a third of the whole class is out of action.

Compare that to the Navy's workhorse destroyers, which have been built in large numbers. Their parts are common. Their crews can move between ships. Their problems have been seen and solved many times over. Being one of many is boring, but boring is cheap. Being one of three is special, and special is expensive.

## Rehook

So the Navy had three advanced ships, with guns that had nothing to fire. What do you do with them? You give them a new job.

## Point seven · A second life

The new plan is to refit the ships with a new kind of long-range hypersonic missile system. Some of the space taken up by the unused guns will be given to the new missiles.

The work in the shipyard is its own kind of irony. Cranes lifting out parts of a gun system that cost a fortune and never got to do its job. Engineers fitting new launch tubes into the space it leaves behind. A ship designed around one idea, being rebuilt around another.

It sounds like a happy ending. The expensive ships finally get a purpose. But this new plan has its own delays. Auditors say the first test firing from one of the ships slipped from twenty twenty-five to twenty twenty-seven. As of August twenty twenty-six, none of the ships had fired the new missile from sea.

And this is an important difference in how we talk about cost. Money spent now on modernising the ships is a new decision. It's not the same as what it cost to build them in the first place. But to the taxpayer, it's all part of the same story: three ships that keep needing more money to do something useful.

## Point eight · Why it happened

There's no single villain in this story. The Zumwalt story is a chain of decisions, each one sensible on its own.

Put lots of new technology into one design, because the future demands it. Cut the number of ships when costs rise, because the budget demands it. Cancel the ammunition when it gets too expensive, because the price demands it. Every step makes sense in a meeting. Put together, they produce three ships, each carrying the cost of a fleet.

## Closing

The Zumwalts aren't a disaster in the way a sunken ship is a disaster. They float, they sail, and they may yet find their purpose. But as a plan, they collapsed. Thirty-two became three. The gun became a guest that never got fed. And the price per ship turned into a number that depends on which question you ask.

Nobody set out to build a ten-billion-dollar destroyer. It happened one reasonable decision at a time, in meeting rooms, over many years.

Somewhere in a Navy shipyard, one of the most futuristic ships ever built is having its guns removed, so it can finally carry something it can use.
