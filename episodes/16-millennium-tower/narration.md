## Hook

One of San Francisco's most expensive apartment towers has sunk about the length of your forearm into the ground, and it's leaning. It opened in two thousand and nine, fifty-eight floors, sold out fast. It stands on piles that stop in sand, without ever reaching the solid rock far below. Seven years after opening, owners learned it had already sunk about sixteen inches. And a hundred-million-dollar fix, designed to stop it, ran into trouble of its own.

## Lock-in

It's called the Millennium Tower. A glass tower in the middle of the city, with luxury apartments and views over the bay. It's become one of the most closely watched buildings in America, by engineers and owners, and by the reporters who keep checking on it. Not for its design, but for a set of numbers measured in inches. So what does it actually mean when a skyscraper sinks and tilts, and why is it so hard to stop?

## Point one · A tower on soft ground

[GAG: the architect builds a sand tower on a beach; it slowly tilts and he props it with one finger]
Rule one of tall buildings: don't build on something softer than the building.

San Francisco sits on rock in some places, and on sand, clay or old landfill in others. Down by the bay, some of the ground under the city was created by people over the last hundred and fifty years. It's softer than it looks. Some of it used to be water.

Big towers in places like that usually reach down to solid rock through long piles, like giant stilts driven deep into the ground. The rock doesn't move, so the building doesn't either. The Millennium Tower stands on piles too, but they reach into a dense layer of sand, not down to the bedrock below.

When the tower opened in twenty o nine, it was a hit. All four hundred and nineteen apartments sold quickly. Buyers paid for the location and the views, and for the prestige of living in one of the city's most talked-about towers.

Picture being one of those first owners. You walk into your new apartment high above the city. Floor-to-ceiling windows, the bay glittering below, the Bay Bridge in the distance. It's the best home you've ever had. And you have no idea the building is slowly moving.

Nobody inside can feel it. A movement of a few inches over several years is far too slow for a person to notice. Doors still close. Floors still look flat. The only way to see it is with precise instruments, measuring the same points again and again, and comparing the numbers.

## Point two · The numbers come out

[GAG: a marble rolls slowly across an apartment floor toward the window]
Estate agent tip: never bring a marble to the viewing.

In twenty sixteen, the news broke. Measurements showed that the tower had sunk about sixteen inches since it was built. And it wasn't sinking evenly. It was leaning, by about two inches at the base, and about six inches at the top.

Sixteen inches is about forty centimetres, in a building that was only seven years old. That's like the ground under your house dropping by the length of your forearm.

But here's an important point that's easy to miss. Sinking and leaning are two different things.

Picture a table with two coins on it, one on each side. If the whole table goes down evenly, both coins drop the same amount. The table is lower, but still flat. Now push down only one side. One coin drops more than the other, and the table tilts.

Buildings are the same. If a tower sinks evenly, that's called settlement, and it's often manageable. If one side sinks more than the other, that's differential settlement, and that's what makes a tower lean. The Millennium Tower was doing both.

And that matters, because the two problems need different fixes. A building that sinks evenly can often live with it, as long as pipes and entrances are adjusted. A building that leans needs the movement on one side to slow down, or the lean will keep growing.

## Rehook

And there's a trick in how leaning shows up. A small tilt at the bottom becomes a much bigger shift at the top.

## Point three · Why the top moves more

Think of a tall stick standing in sand. Push the base a tiny bit to one side, and the tip at the top swings much further. The angle is the same. The distance is not.

That's why reports about the tower often talk about the lean at the roof. A couple of inches at the base turns into many inches at the top, fifty-eight floors up. It sounds alarming. It's also simple geometry.

That's why it's important to know which number a headline is quoting. The lean at the roof will always be the biggest, scariest number. The tilt at the base is smaller, but it's the one that tells you what the foundation is doing. Both are true, and both are measured, but they answer different questions.

In twenty seventeen, city inspectors said the building was safe to occupy, with no evidence of life-safety concerns. People kept living there, going to work and coming home. But the movement didn't stop. By twenty twenty-one, the tower had sunk about eighteen inches in total, and the lean at the top had reached about twenty-six inches. At the time, the tilt at the top was growing by around three inches a year.

Now put yourself in an owner's position, reading those headlines. You haven't moved. Your apartment looks the same. But every few months, there's a new number about your building in the news, and the number keeps getting bigger.

## Point four · The fix

So the engineers came up with a plan. Add new piles around the edge of the tower, driven all the way down to bedrock, and connect them to the building. Then gradually transfer some of the tower's weight from the soft ground onto those new supports. The idea was not to push the building back upright. It was to stop it from moving further, by giving it a new set of legs that reach something solid.

The fix was estimated to cost around one hundred million dollars. The first version of the plan called for fifty-two new piles. It was later scaled back to eighteen.

And then something ironic happened. In twenty twenty-one, while crews were drilling the new piles, the tower sank another inch or so. The work that was meant to stop the movement seemed to be speeding it up. The project was paused while engineers worked out why.

The likely problem was the drilling itself. Pushing new piles down through soft, wet ground next to a heavy building can disturb the soil the building is already resting on. Engineers had to change how the work was done, so the cure wouldn't make the illness worse.

## Point five · Measuring a moving target

The work resumed, and the new piles were installed. In twenty twenty-three, the weight of the building was transferred onto them in stages, on specific dates, step by step.

And here's where measurement becomes everything. The city publishes regular monitoring reports, numbered one after another, for anyone to read. Instruments at named points around the building are checked again and again. One report from June twenty twenty-four shows the changes between May twenty twenty-one and June twenty twenty-four.

That date range is important. A number that covers three years is not the total movement since twenty o nine. When you read a headline about the tower, the first question should always be: measured from when, to when? And from which point? A corner, the roof, the base? Each one tells a slightly different story, and a careful reader asks for all three.

After the upgrade, engineers acknowledged that the tilt improved less than they had hoped in the first months. Then, after some early progress, it started leaning further west again.

## Rehook

So is the tower collapsing? No. That's the strange part, and it's why the story is so easy to get wrong. The collapse here isn't the building. It's the promise that came with it: that a brand new luxury tower would stay exactly where it was built.

## Point six · Living in a measured building

[GAG: at a party, a guest asks where the architect lives; he points at the leaning tower; everyone takes a step back]
"Where do you live?" "Oh, the leaning one." Instant conversation stopper.

Owning an apartment here means living in a building with its own monitoring reports. Engineers' charts with dates, points and arrows. Every report is public. Every new number is a headline.

For owners, that's a hard way to live. Even if the building is safe, the value of the apartments depends on what buyers believe about the future. And beliefs move with the news. A reported change of an inch can feel like a disaster, even when it's a small change over a long time.

That's why the honest way to talk about the tower is with dates and measurements, not adjectives. Not "it's sinking fast" or "it's fine." But: between this date and that date, this point moved this much.

That's less exciting than a headline. But it's the only kind of sentence that's actually useful to someone who lives there, wants to buy there, or wants to know whether the fix is working.

## Point seven · Why it's so hard

So why can't engineers just straighten it? Because a tower of this size weighs a huge amount, and the soil underneath it has been squeezed by that weight for years. Any work underneath it, like drilling new piles, disturbs the ground and can make it move. Fixing a foundation under a finished, occupied skyscraper is like changing the legs of a table while people are eating dinner on it. Every move has to be small, and every one of them gets watched.

And the soil keeps responding slowly, for years. A fix that works in the first month may look different after five years. That's why the monitoring continues long after the construction crews have gone.

And there's one more twist. A tower doesn't have to move much to cause a big problem for the people who own it. Even a small, slow movement can scare buyers, lower prices and lead to years of arguments. The ground moved inches. The value of the apartments moved much more.

## Closing

The ground under this tower was always going to have a say. It was built tall on ground that kept moving, on foundations that didn't reach the rock. Once it was finished and full of people, every fix became harder, slower and riskier than it would have been before the first floor went up.

The Millennium Tower is still standing, still lived in and still measured. It's a reminder that a building isn't finished the day it opens. Every building has a conversation with the ground under it. Most of the time, that conversation is quiet. Here, it's been measured, reported and argued about for years. It keeps a relationship with the ground for as long as it stands.

Somewhere in downtown San Francisco, instruments are checking a glass tower again, point by point, date by date, to see how far it has moved since the last time someone looked.
