## Hook

In nineteen fifty-nine, the US Army built a town inside the Greenland ice sheet. Tunnels cut into the ice, buildings inside the tunnels, and a nuclear reactor to power it all. Eight years later, they walked away and left it there. And the town was a test for something far bigger: a plan to hide up to six hundred nuclear missiles in tunnels under the ice. The full story only came out in nineteen ninety-five. In twenty twenty-four, a NASA plane flew over the spot, and its radar found the base, still down there, buried under more than thirty metres of ice.

## Lock-in

It was called Camp Century, and for a few years, it was one of the strangest addresses on Earth. Officially, it was a research station showing that people could live and work deep in the Arctic, far from any town or road. Behind it, there was a much bigger and much more secret idea. Both of them failed for the same reason: the ice wouldn't sit still. And what the Army left behind is now slowly travelling with the ice, toward a future nobody can fully predict. So how do you build a town inside a glacier, and why do you leave it there?

## Point one · A town under the snow

[GAG: the architect jogs along an ice tunnel in shorts and freezes mid-stride]
Great location, very private, a bit draughty.

Picture northwest Greenland in the late nineteen fifties. A white plain of ice that goes on forever, flat in every direction, with nothing to block the wind. Winds that can knock you off your feet. Months of darkness in winter, with temperatures far below freezing.

The Army's idea was bold. Instead of building on top of the ice, where storms would bury everything, they would build inside it. Machines cut long trenches into the ice sheet. Arched roofs were put over the trenches, and snow piled on top. And inside those tunnels, they put prefabricated buildings, like a row of cabins in a very long, very cold corridor.

There were twenty-one tunnels in total, about three kilometres of them. Inside, there were places to sleep, eat and work. Labs. Workshops. A small hospital area. A whole town, under the snow.

And people really lived there. Soldiers, engineers and scientists, often around a couple of hundred at a time, rotating in and out. They slept in bunks, ate in a mess hall, worked shifts, and walked to their jobs through corridors of ice. For them, this wasn't a secret experiment. It was where they woke up every morning.

Imagine walking down the main tunnel. A ceiling of packed snow above you. Lights strung along the walls. Wooden buildings on either side, with doors and windows, and warm rooms behind them. And just beyond the walls of the tunnel, solid ice in every direction.

## Point two · A reactor in the Arctic

[GAG: the architect plugs a toaster into a nuclear reactor]
Most towns have a power station. This one had a nuclear reactor down the corridor. Very fifties.

A town needs power. Lots of it, especially in a place that cold. Flying in fuel for heaters and generators would be expensive and slow.

Every litre of fuel would have to travel a very long way. By ship to the coast, then across the ice by tractor train, through storms, for days. Each delivery was an expedition. The more fuel the base burned, the more expeditions it needed.

So Camp Century got something no Arctic base had before. A portable nuclear reactor, called the PM-2A. It was described as the world's first portable nuclear reactor, shipped in pieces and assembled on site. From nineteen sixty, it provided power and heat to the base.

That's the kind of detail that sounds unbelievable today. A nuclear reactor, inside tunnels, inside an ice sheet, in the Arctic. In the confident spirit of the time, it was a display piece. Proof that modern technology could tame even the harshest place on Earth.

But the reactor didn't stay long. The same thing that made the base possible also made it hard to keep, and the reactor was one of the first big pieces to go. It ran for about three years, until nineteen sixty-three, and then it was shut down and removed.

## Rehook

And here's the problem nobody could engineer away. Ice looks solid. It isn't.

## Point three · The ice that moves

[GAG: the architect shaves the tunnel wall with a giant razor; overnight the ice grows back]
Every day you shave the walls, and every night the ice grows a beard.

A glacier is not a block of stone. It flows, slowly, like extremely thick honey. Under its own enormous weight, the ice creeps and squeezes and deforms.

So the tunnels of Camp Century didn't stay the same shape. The walls and ceilings slowly pressed inward. Crews had to keep cutting the ice back, again and again, just to keep the tunnels open. It was like living in a house where the walls move a few centimetres closer every year, and you have to keep shaving them away.

On top of that, snow kept falling on the surface, and in Greenland, it never really melts away. Every year, a new layer piled up above the base. The town was slowly sinking deeper under the ice sheet, without moving an inch on its own.

For the soldiers stationed there, this was daily life. Your job isn't just to live and work. Part of your job is to fight the ice, every day, to keep the town from closing up around you.

And the fight costs more every year. More cutting, more shovelling, more repairs to buildings that slowly get squeezed out of shape. A base like this isn't built once. It's rebuilt, a little at a time, forever. The moment you stop, the ice starts to win.

## Point four · The secret plan

[GAG: the architect unrolls a tunnel map that keeps unrolling across the room and out of the door]
When the army thinks big, it thinks Denmark-times-three big.

Now, here's the part that stayed secret for decades. Camp Century was also a test for a much bigger idea, called Project Iceworm.

The proposal was to use tunnels like these on a massive scale, spreading them under the ice and hiding up to six hundred nuclear missiles inside. The network would have covered an area of about fifty-two thousand square miles, roughly three times the size of Denmark. The details only came to light in nineteen ninety-five, during an inquiry in Denmark.

But the ice that made Camp Century hard to maintain made Iceworm impossible. If the tunnels of one small town needed constant cutting to stay open, a giant network was never going to work. The plan was cancelled in the nineteen sixties.

Think about the difference in scale. One small town, a few hundred people, already fighting the ice every day. Now picture a network many, many times bigger, which has to stay perfectly stable for years. The ice was never going to allow it.

It's important to keep these two things apart. Camp Century was real. Iceworm was a proposal that never got built. The ice stopped the big plan before it started, and it slowly wore down the small one.

## Point five · The science that survived

Not everything at Camp Century was about the military. Scientists there drilled deep into the ice and pulled up long cylinders called ice cores.

Ice cores are like a diary of the climate. Every year's snow traps tiny bubbles of air and traces of dust. Layer by layer, going down, you go back in time. The cores from Camp Century became some of the most important early records of Earth's past climate, and researchers still cite them today.

Picture a scientist in one of those labs, holding a cylinder of ice that fell as snow centuries ago. Inside it, tiny bubbles of ancient air. Every one of them is a sample of an atmosphere nobody alive has ever breathed.

So in a strange way, the most lasting thing to come out of a secret military base was a piece of climate science.

## Rehook

In nineteen sixty-seven, the Army left Camp Century for good. But it didn't take everything with it.

## Point six · What was left behind

[GAG: the architect switches off the lights in the tunnel and snow closes over the entrance]
Out of sight, out of mind. Until the climate changes its mind.

When the base was abandoned, much of what was inside stayed in the ice. Buildings. Equipment. Fuel. And waste, including waste from the time the reactor ran.

Someone had to be the last person to leave the tunnels. You switch off the lights in a corridor of ice. You close the doors on rooms where people lived for years. And you walk out, knowing the snow will close over all of it.

The thinking back then was simple. Snow would keep falling. The base would be buried deeper and deeper. And the ice would keep it all locked away forever.

In twenty sixteen, a team of scientists led by William Colgan looked at that assumption. They estimated what was down there, including around two hundred thousand litres of diesel, spread over a site of about fifty-five hectares. And they asked a question nobody asked in the nineteen sixties. What if the climate changes?

They found that if warming continues, melting at the surface could eventually start to reach the buried base, possibly by around the end of this century. Not tomorrow. Not certainly. But no longer never.

That's a strange kind of collapse. Nothing has broken yet. Nothing has leaked into the open. But the assumption the whole plan rested on, that the ice would stay forever, has stopped being safe.

## Point seven · Seeing it from the air

In April twenty twenty-four, a NASA plane flew over the area, mapping the ice with radar. And the radar picked up the base. The tunnels, the structures, still there, buried under at least thirty metres of ice and snow.

It's worth knowing what that image really is. It's not a photograph. Radar sends signals into the ice and listens to what bounces back. Buildings and tunnels reflect differently from plain ice. So the picture is a kind of echo map, a shadow of the town that the ice has swallowed.

And here's why it matters. The depth of the base today doesn't, on its own, tell you when or whether it will come back to the surface. That depends on how much snow falls, how much ice melts, and how the ice moves. That's why scientists keep measuring. It's a monitoring question, not a countdown clock.

And there's another question hidden in all this. Whose problem is it? The base was built by one country, on land that is part of another, under an agreement made in a very different world. When something left behind decades ago starts to matter again, someone has to take responsibility for it. That conversation is still going on.

## Closing

The ice was never solid ground. The Army treated it like it was. It moved, it squeezed the tunnels, and it buried the town a little deeper every year. The big secret plan died because of it. The small town was worn down by it.

And then the Army made a second bet on the same ice: that it would hold everything left behind, forever. That bet is still running.

There's a lesson in that for every project that leaves something behind. Forever is a very long time, and the world it assumes doesn't always last.

Somewhere under the Greenland ice sheet, a town from nineteen fifty-nine is still down there, in the dark, moving very slowly with the ice. Scientists are watching it from above, with radar, satellites and measurements of the snow. And the ice will decide the rest.
