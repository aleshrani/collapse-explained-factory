## Hook

In the hills of Turkey, there's a town made of more than five hundred little castles. Pointy towers, round turrets, tiny balconies, all exactly the same. Each one was supposed to sell for hundreds of thousands of dollars. And today, nobody lives in any of them.

[SHORT 1 (A): start "In the hills of Turkey, there's a town made of more than five hundred little castles." end-after "nobody lives in any of them." loop "So who wanted to buy a castle that looks exactly like the one next door?"]

## Lock-in

The place is called Burj Al Babas. Photos of it go around the internet every few months, and people always ask the same thing. Is this real? It is. It was meant to be a luxury resort for wealthy foreign buyers. Instead, it became one of the strangest ghost towns on Earth. So how do you build hundreds of castles and end up with a fairy-tale village that nobody moves into?

## Point one · The plan

[GAG: the architect at a sales desk wearing a paper crown, fanning out identical brochures]
Why buy a house when you can buy a castle? The exact same castle as your neighbour's. And his neighbour's.

The site sits in a green valley near the small town of Mudurnu, in northwest Turkey. It's a pretty area of forests and hills, a few hours' drive from Istanbul and Ankara. The kind of place where city people go to breathe cleaner air for a weekend. Quiet roads, old villages, and summers that are a lot cooler than in many parts of the Middle East.

The plan was big. Seven hundred and thirty-two villas. Every single one designed like a miniature French château, with steep grey roofs, little towers and arched windows. Around them, a shopping mall and a hotel. A whole resort town, built from scratch, in one style.

And the buyers the developers had in mind weren't local families. They were mostly wealthy buyers from the Gulf, people who might want a holiday home in a cooler, greener place for the hot summer months. The villas were priced at roughly three hundred and seventy thousand to half a million dollars each.

Picture the brochure. A castle of your own, in the forest, with towers and a view of the hills. Not a flat in a tower block. A castle. For a certain kind of buyer, that's a hard picture to say no to.

And the idea has a simple logic. People already pay a lot for something that looks special. A house is a house, but a castle is a story you can tell your friends. If you can build that story cheaply and sell it at a premium, hundreds of times over, you've found a very good business. At least, that's how it looks before the first brick goes in.

## Point two · Why they all look the same

[GAG: a pizza delivery driver stares at a map where every pin points to an identical castle]
Pizza delivery here must be a nightmare.

Here's the first thing everybody notices in the photos. The castles are identical. Not similar. Identical. Row after row after row, the same tower, the same roof, the same windows, lined up in neat curving streets.

From a builder's point of view, that makes sense. You design one house and repeat it hundreds of times. One set of drawings. One set of moulds. The same windows ordered in bulk. Workers learn the job once and do it again and again. On paper, it's the cheapest way to build a lot of houses quickly.

Picture the building site in its busiest days. Trucks lined up on the valley road. Concrete pumps working on one row of foundations while another row gets its walls. Further along, workers fitting the same pointed roofs onto the same towers, one after another, like an assembly line that happens to be outdoors. From a distance, you can watch the castles appear, street by street.

But there's a catch hiding in that logic. When you repeat a design, you repeat the savings, but you also repeat every cost. If the price of steel, glass or roofing goes up, it goes up seven hundred and thirty-two times. And if one thing about the design doesn't sell, it doesn't sell seven hundred and thirty-two times either.

Now picture walking down one of those streets. Castle on the left. Castle on the right. Castle ahead, castle behind. After a few minutes, you start to lose track of which one you've already passed. A delivery driver would need the house number, because the house itself tells you nothing.

## Rehook

For a while, it worked. The castles went up, one after another. Hundreds of them. And then the money started to move in the wrong direction.

## Point three · When the currency falls

[GAG: a seesaw: the lira sinks on one side, the price tag of a castle shoots up on the other]
The currency went down and the costs went up. The worst seesaw in the playground.

Here's a problem every builder with big dreams eventually meets. A house takes time to build. Months, sometimes years. And during that time, the world doesn't stand still.

In twenty eighteen, the value of the Turkish lira fell sharply. For a developer, that's a nightmare. Many building materials and costs are tied to foreign prices. So when your currency loses value, the same steel and the same fittings suddenly cost a lot more in lira. On top of that, the project had faced delays with permits, and every month of delay adds to the bill.

Now imagine you're the developer. You've already started hundreds of houses. You've promised buyers a price. And every week, the cost of finishing each castle goes up. You can't stop halfway without leaving half-built houses. And you can't easily raise the price on people who already signed.

This is the part that rarely makes it into the viral videos. A project like this doesn't usually die in one dramatic moment. It dies slowly, in spreadsheets. Each month, the gap between what it costs to finish and what you'll be paid gets a little wider. At some point, the gap is bigger than anyone is willing to cover.

By the time work stopped, around five hundred and eighty-seven villas had been built or partly built. Not seven hundred and thirty-two. And partly built is the key phrase. Many of them had walls and roofs, but not everything a family needs to actually move in.

## Point four · Bankruptcy

In twenty eighteen, the developer tried to restructure its debts through the courts. The court refused that plan. In November twenty eighteen, it ordered bankruptcy instead.

And with that, the building sites went quiet. The cranes stopped. The workers left. Hundreds of castles stood in rows on the hillside, some finished on the outside, many empty inside, with nobody living in any of them.

Imagine being one of the last workers to leave. You've spent months building the same tower over and over. You know every corner of it. And now you drive out of the valley, past hundreds of houses you helped build, knowing that not one of them has a family waiting to move in.

Early in twenty nineteen, there was news that the project had won a reprieve. Hope came back for a moment. But the valley didn't fill up with families. The castles stayed empty.

## Rehook

Here's the strangest part. The town wasn't abandoned because it was badly built, or because something terrible happened there. It was abandoned because the numbers stopped working. And numbers are invisible in a photo.

## Point five · The difference between a house and a home

[GAG: the architect knocks on a castle door and the door falls flat onto the floor]
Fairy-tale castle. Not fairy-tale plumbing.

From a drone, Burj Al Babas looks almost finished. Grey roofs, white walls, neat streets. It looks like a resort waiting for its opening day.

But a house you can photograph and a house you can live in are two very different things. To move in, you need water that comes out of the tap. Electricity that's connected. A road that's finished. Sewers. Heating. Windows in every frame. Someone to keep the lights on in the shopping centre.

And here's the hidden problem with a resort. The fancy shared things, the mall, the hotel, the shops, only make sense when enough people live there. A shop with no customers closes. A hotel with no guests can't pay its staff. So the first buyers wait for everyone else, and everyone else waits for the first buyers.

That's a trap that has nothing to do with castles. It happens to every new town, every new resort and every new district. Somebody has to go first. And nobody wants to be the only family living in a fairy-tale town with no neighbours.

## Point six · The ghost town that went viral

[GAG: the architect launches a drone; fifty other drones appear over the castles]
As a resort, it flopped. As a drone video, it's a hit.

Today, the castles are still there. Rows of grey towers in a green valley, unfinished and unoccupied. Visitors and photographers come to walk the empty streets. Drone videos of it collect millions of views. People who will never buy a castle are fascinated by a town that nobody lives in.

Walking there must feel like stepping into a video game that hasn't loaded yet. Perfect shapes, repeating forever, with no people, no cars, no washing on the balconies. Just the wind, the towers and the sound of your own footsteps between the walls.

That's the strange afterlife of a failed project. As a business, it collapsed. As an image, it became famous. The castles finally got the attention the developers wanted. Just not from buyers.

As for what happens next, nobody can say for sure. Nobody knows what happens next. Every few years, there's talk of a new plan, a new investor, a new chance. And in the meantime, the castles wait.

Weather doesn't wait, though. Every winter of rain and snow on an unfinished building does a little more damage. Open window frames let water in. Unsealed walls crack. A castle that stands empty for years needs more work, not less, before anyone can live in it. The longer the wait, the more expensive the rescue.

## Closing

Nobody can blame the towers or the turrets. The real enemy was time. A project this big takes years, and in those years, the currency moved, the costs moved and the buyers' plans moved. Repeating one design seven hundred and thirty-two times multiplied the savings, but it also multiplied the risk.

The developers sold a dream that looked perfect in a brochure: your own castle in the forest. But a dream needs neighbours, shops, roads and water. And none of those can be repeated from a single drawing.

There's also a lesson about who a project is built for. These castles were designed for a very specific buyer, from a specific region, with a specific budget and a specific taste. When that buyer hesitated, there was no plan B. A row of normal houses might have found other buyers. A row of seven hundred identical castles was always going to need exactly the people it was built for.

If you ever see a photo of it, look closely at the windows. In a town where people live, every window is a little different. Curtains, plants, a light left on. Here, every window is the same, because behind every one of them, there's nobody.

Somewhere in the hills near Mudurnu, hundreds of identical castles are still standing in their rows. Every one of them has a front door. None of them has a family behind it.
