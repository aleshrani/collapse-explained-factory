## Hook

In nineteen seventy-four, the CIA sent a giant ship into the middle of the Pacific Ocean and told the world it was mining the seabed. It wasn't. Its real job was to reach down almost three miles and pull up a Soviet submarine. And the whole secret came apart because of a burglary in Los Angeles.

## Lock-in

This is one of the most expensive, most secret and most ambitious recovery missions ever attempted. It needed a billionaire's name, a fake industry and a machine nobody had ever built before. It almost worked. And when it failed, it left behind something that outlived the ship itself: four words the government still uses every time it doesn't want to answer a question. So how do you fake an entire mining industry to steal a submarine?

## Point one · A submarine that vanished

In nineteen sixty-eight, a Soviet submarine called K-129 went out into the Pacific and never came back. It sank with its whole crew. The Soviet Navy searched for it and couldn't find it. Somewhere out there, a whole crew had disappeared, and their families were left with no wreck, no answers and no grave.

The Americans could. Their underwater listening systems had picked up sounds from the area, and after a long search, they located the wreck. It was lying on the bottom, more than sixteen thousand feet down. That's nearly five kilometers of water above it. Almost three miles.

To understand how deep that is, take more than half of Mount Everest and turn it upside down in the ocean. The pressure down there is crushing. Sunlight never reaches it. Nobody had ever lifted anything that big from that deep.

But this wasn't just any wreck. A Soviet submarine could hold secrets the Americans badly wanted. Codes, equipment, the kind of things spy agencies dream about. So somebody at the CIA asked a question that sounds completely insane. What if we just go and pick it up?

## Point two · The ship that couldn't exist

[GAG: a shipyard worker points at a giant claw on the crane; the architect whispers in his ear]
"What's the claw for?" "Rocks." "Rocks?" "Really big rocks."

To lift a submarine from three miles down, you need a ship nobody has ever built. A ship with a tower in the middle, like an oil rig. A pipe made of steel sections that could be lowered, joint by joint, all the way to the bottom. At the end of that pipe, a giant claw that could grab the wreck. And in the bottom of the ship, a secret pool with doors that open to the sea, so the prize could be pulled inside without anyone watching.

And it would have to be huge. The finished ship was more than six hundred feet long, about the length of two football fields, with a tower rising from its middle like a drilling rig out at sea.

Building that is hard. Building it in secret is harder. Shipyards are full of workers. Steel orders leave paperwork. Satellites and fishing boats can see you at sea. Anyone looking at a ship like that would ask the obvious question. What is it for?

So the CIA needed an answer. A story so believable that nobody would look twice. Not a lie that would hold up for a day. A lie big enough to explain a giant ship, a crew of specialists and years of strange engineering, and boring enough that nobody would bother to check it.

## Point three · The billionaire cover story

[GAG: the architect at a press conference in a hard hat, proudly holding up a lump of rock]
Never in history has anyone been this excited about rocks.

The answer was Howard Hughes. Billionaire, aviator, film producer, famously eccentric and famously secretive. If anyone in America could suddenly decide to build a strange giant ship for a brand new business, it was him.

The business was deep-sea mining. At the time, people were excited about lumps of metal-rich rock lying on the ocean floor, called manganese nodules. The idea was that one day, ships would scoop them up and make a fortune. Nobody was doing it at scale yet. So a weird new ship with a giant tower and lots of pipe looked exactly like what a pioneer in that industry would build.

The ship got its name. The Hughes Glomar Explorer. Glomar came from Global Marine, the company commissioned to build it. Newspapers wrote about the future of ocean mining. There was a whole public conversation about an industry that, for this ship, was only a disguise.

Imagine being an engineer on that project. You're designing the most advanced lifting system on Earth, and you can't tell your own family what it's really for. At parties, you talk about rocks on the seabed. At work, you're planning to lift a submarine.

## Rehook

In the summer of nineteen seventy-four, the ship sailed. And on the fourth of July, it arrived over the wreck.

## Point four · Three miles of pipe

Now the hardest part began. The crew lowered the pipe, section after section, down through the dark water. Three miles of steel hanging from one ship, with the claw at the end, swinging in currents nobody could see.

Then the claw reached the bottom. It closed around the submarine. And very slowly, the whole system began to lift.

Picture the weight. A big chunk of a submarine, full of water, hanging from a pipe that's miles long. Every meter up is a fight against pressure, gravity and metal that has spent six years at the bottom of the ocean. On the deck, people are watching gauges, waiting.

And then, partway up, the claw failed. Parts of it broke. Most of the submarine slipped out and fell back into the dark, all the way down to the seafloor.

What stayed in the claw was a forward section, around thirty-eight feet of it. The rest of the prize was gone.

Imagine being on that deck. Six years of planning, a secret ship, a fake industry, and a billionaire's name on the hull. All of it came down to one moment, three miles below you, where a piece of metal you couldn't see decided to break. There was no second chance on that trip. Whatever fell back was going to stay on the bottom of the Pacific.

## Point five · What they found

What exactly the Americans recovered from that piece is still not fully public. The official accounts say only part of the submarine was retrieved, and that the most valuable parts sank with the rest.

But they did recover something else. The remains of Soviet sailors who had died in the submarine. And here's a moment in this story that's easy to forget. The crew of the Glomar Explorer held a burial at sea for them. A spy ship, on a secret mission against the Soviet Union, stopped to give the enemy's sailors a funeral.

The ceremony was even filmed, so that one day, the other side could see that its men had been treated with respect.

It's one of the strangest images of the whole Cold War. An American crew standing on the deck of a fake mining ship, in the middle of the Pacific, burying men from the other side with ceremony.

## Rehook

So the mission was a partial failure, but at least the secret was safe. For a few months, it was. And then the secret started leaking from the most unlikely place.

## Point six · The burglary

On the fifth of June, nineteen seventy-four, a month before the ship even reached the wreck, burglars broke into an office building owned by Howard Hughes on Romaine Street, in Los Angeles. They stole cash. And they stole papers.

At first, it looked like an ordinary break-in at a rich man's office. But among those papers, there may have been documents that linked Hughes, the CIA and the ship. Now the government had a terrifying problem. Somewhere out there, someone might be holding the key to its biggest secret.

The pieces came together in the press. On the seventh of February, nineteen seventy-five, the Los Angeles Times published a story connecting the burglary, Hughes, the CIA and a secret recovery operation. The CIA later said the story was wrong in important facts. But it was enough. In March, the journalist Jack Anderson broadcast it nationally.

Behind the scenes, the CIA tried to hold it back. Its director personally asked news organizations to sit on the story, arguing that publishing it could damage national security. For a while, some of them agreed. But a secret that many editors know is a secret with a timer on it.

The cover was blown. The giant mining ship had never mined anything. And any hope of going back for the rest of the submarine sank with it.

## Point seven · Neither confirm nor deny

[GAG: a reporter holds out a microphone; the architect stands behind a podium with a straight face]
"Did you build a ship to steal a submarine?" "We can neither confirm nor deny." "Is today Tuesday?" "We can neither confirm nor deny."

Here's the twist that outlived everything. A journalist named Harriet Ann Phillippi asked the CIA for records about the ship, using America's freedom of information law. The CIA's answer became famous. It said it could neither confirm nor deny that such records even existed.

Think about how strange that is. By then, the story had been in the newspapers and on national radio. Millions of people had heard about the ship. Not "no." Not "yes, but it's secret." Just: we won't even tell you whether the thing you're asking about exists.

Phillippi took it to court. In nineteen seventy-six, the court accepted the CIA's position. And that kind of answer got a name, after the ship. It's called the Glomar response. Government agencies still use it today, decades later, whenever they want to refuse to say whether a record exists.

## Point eight · The ship that finally got a real job

[GAG: the old ship finally drills into the seabed; the architect gives it a proud thumbs up]
Forty years of pretending to be a mining ship, and then it actually became one. Method acting.

The Glomar Explorer didn't sink into history. After the mission, it sat around for years, a giant ship built for one secret job. Later, it was converted into what it had only pretended to be: a working ship for the ocean industry. It became a deep-sea drilling ship.

And in twenty fifteen, more than forty years after its secret mission, its owner announced it would be scrapped. On the fifth of June, twenty fifteen, it arrived at the ship breakers in China. Forty-one years to the day after the burglary that blew its cover.

The CIA officially confirmed the project's name, Azorian, only in twenty ten, when it released its own internal history of the mission. Thirty-six years after the claw broke, the agency finally put its own version of the story on paper for the public to read. Even then, large parts of it were blacked out.

## Closing

Here's how close it came. The engineering almost worked. A machine reached three miles down and grabbed a submarine, something no one had ever done. But the claw broke at the worst possible moment, and the secret broke on the ground, in a burglary that had nothing to do with the sea.

The fake mining ship ended its life as a real ocean worker. The submarine is still mostly on the bottom of the Pacific. And every time a government says it can neither confirm nor deny, it's quoting a ship that pretended to look for rocks.
