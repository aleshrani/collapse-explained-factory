## Hook

In twenty twenty, the singer Akon stood on a stretch of coast in Senegal and laid the first stone of a six-billion-dollar city. The internet called it a real-life Wakanda. A year later, people went back to the site and found grass. And in twenty twenty-five, the city was officially cancelled. Out of eight hundred hectares, eight were left for a new, much smaller plan.

## Lock-in

Akon City was one of the most talked-about city projects of its time. Futuristic towers, a celebrity founder, its own cryptocurrency, and a promise to put a small coastal village on the world map. This is the story of how all of that turned into an empty field, a basketball court and a much smaller resort plan. So how does a six-billion-dollar city disappear before a single tower goes up?

## Point one · A city from a song

[GAG: the architect unrolls a rendering of curvy towers so long it rolls out of the frame]
Six billion dollars, curving towers and its own cryptocurrency. Usually you start with a sewer pipe, but sure.

Akon is one of the best-known musicians to come out of Senegal's diaspora. He grew up partly in Senegal and partly in the United States, and his family has roots there. By twenty eighteen, he was talking about something much bigger than music: a city.

The plan was for a futuristic city on the coast near a village called Mbodiène, south of the capital Dakar. On a site of about eight hundred hectares. That's roughly eleven hundred football pitches of land.

The pictures were spectacular. Curving towers, white and gold buildings and lots of glass, with greenery everywhere and a skyline that looked more like science fiction than West Africa. There would be hotels, offices and homes, and the plans talked about tourism on a big scale. And the whole thing was priced at around six billion dollars.

And then there was the currency. Akon said the city would run on his own cryptocurrency, called Akoin. A new city, with its own money, built by a pop star. You can see why the headlines wrote themselves.

Now put yourself in the position of a possible investor. You're being asked to put money into a city that doesn't exist, on land that's empty, paid for in a currency that's brand new, led by someone whose main career is music. The pictures are beautiful. The founder is famous. But your job is to ask a boring question: who pays for the first road, and when do they get their money back?

## Point two · The real-life Wakanda

Almost immediately, people compared it to Wakanda, the high-tech African nation from the Marvel films. For many people, especially in the African diaspora, that comparison meant something. Here was a vision of an African city that looked like the future, not like a stereotype.

That's the power of a celebrity project. It doesn't need a decade of planning meetings to get attention. One video, one rendering, one famous face, and suddenly the whole world is talking about a village most people had never heard of.

And there was something genuinely exciting about it. A city designed and promoted by an African star, built on African land, and aimed at tourists and investors from around the world. For a lot of people, that was the point of the whole project. Buildings were only half of it. The other half was about who gets to dream up the future, and whose cities get to look like tomorrow.

But attention isn't money. And a city needs a lot of money, poured in steadily for many years, before anything appears on the ground.

## Point three · The first stone

[GAG: after the ceremony, the stone sits alone in a field; a goat walks up and sniffs it]
The first stone of a six-billion-dollar city. The goat was not impressed.

In twenty twenty, the project had its big ceremonial moment. Akon, officials and guests gathered at the site to lay the first stone. Cameras, speeches, a symbolic start to a six-billion-dollar dream.

Picture that day. A stretch of flat land near the ocean. A stage, chairs, a crowd. A single stone being placed in the ground, and around it, as far as you can see, open grassland. That stone is supposed to be the first piece of a skyline.

Ceremonies like this are common in big projects. They mark a start, attract press and reassure everyone involved. But a ceremony is a promise, not a schedule. What matters is what happens the week after the cameras leave. Do the trucks arrive? Does anyone start digging?

The stone was the easy part. Now someone had to pay for roads, water, power, sewers, foundations, workers and materials, in a place where almost none of that existed yet.

And here's where the story turns. Because a year after the ceremony, journalists went back to look.

## Point four · A year of grass

In September twenty twenty-one, a year after the ceremony, the site was still grassland. No towers, no construction crews, no futuristic skyline. And the people living nearby said they had no idea what was happening.

Put yourself in the shoes of one of the villagers. A year ago, your quiet stretch of coast was on the news all over the world. A famous singer promised jobs, tourism and a city of the future, right next door. You'd heard that land had been set aside. Maybe you'd even made plans around it. And then, month after month, nothing happens. No trucks. No workers. No explanation.

That's one of the hardest parts of a stalled megaproject. A stalled project disappoints investors, and it also freezes a whole community in a kind of waiting room. Do you plan for the city, or for the village you already live in?

## Point five · What actually got built

[GAG: the architect shoots hoops on a basketball court in the middle of an empty field]
Promised: the city of the future. Delivered: a pretty decent basketball court.

Something did get built, eventually. But not a city. Reportedly, Akon's own money went into a few smaller things near the site: a youth centre, a basketball court, and an information centre about the project.

Think about the scale of that. The promise was a six-billion-dollar city. The visible result, years later, was a building where you could learn about the city that wasn't there.

Picture walking into that information centre. Posters of curving towers on the walls. Models of the skyline, maybe a video playing on a loop. And then you walk back outside, and the view is the same open land it was before the first stone. The future is inside the building. The present is outside it.

To be fair, big projects often start slowly. Land has to be prepared. Investors have to sign. Permissions have to be granted. But there's a point where slow becomes stuck, and the people watching can tell the difference.

## Point six · The final notice

[GAG: a formal letter slides across a desk]
"Dear Sir. Regarding your city. Please start building it. Kind regards, the government."

The land belonged to a deal with a state company called SAPCO, which develops Senegal's coastal and tourism areas. And by twenty twenty-four, SAPCO's patience was running out.

In August twenty twenty-four, it gave formal notice. Unless work on the project started, the state would take back about ninety percent of the land.

That's a remarkable moment. A government that had welcomed a celebrity city with open arms was now sending him a final warning. The dream had become a deadline.

From the state's point of view, the logic is simple. Coastal land suitable for tourism is valuable. Leaving it frozen for years under a project that isn't moving means losing other chances to develop it. Every year of waiting has a cost, even when nothing is being built.

## Point seven · Cancelled

In twenty twenty-five, Akon City was officially dropped. The original city project, the head of SAPCO said, was no longer current. In its place, there would be a scaled-back tourist resort, overseen by the state company. Lack of funding and stalled construction were among the reasons given.

And Akon himself? He kept eight hectares of the original land, for a new, separate project that was still being finalised.

Eight hectares sounds like a lot until you compare it. The original site was eight hundred. So eight hectares is one percent. It's about eleven football pitches. Big enough for a resort or a development, but nowhere near a city. And remember the difference between land and buildings. Eight hectares of land is a plot, not a finished project. So far, nobody can point to a skyline there.

## Point eight · What a city actually needs

[GAG: the architect stacks shiny tower models on bare sand; they wobble; he slides a pipe underneath first]
Rule one of building a city: the boring stuff goes in first.

Here's what the beautiful pictures never showed. A city isn't towers. Towers are the last thing you see, and usually the last thing you build. A city is pipes, power lines, roads, drainage, schools, clinics and, above all, people with a reason to live there. Before the first tower can rise, someone has to build all the boring things underneath it.

For a site that starts as open land on the coast, that's an enormous bill. It needs steady funding over many years, from investors who believe they'll get their money back. A famous name can open doors. It can't replace that kind of money.

And a futuristic design adds its own problem. Curving, unusual towers are much harder and more expensive to build than ordinary buildings. They make great images. They make expensive construction sites.

There's also a question of trust. Investors want to see a track record. Has this team built something like this before? Who are the engineers? Which banks are involved? A celebrity founder can bring attention, but for a six-billion-dollar city, the people with the money will always want to see the people with the experience.

## Closing

Akon City's real problem was timing. The idea arrived years before the money. Akon City had the attention of the world from the very first video, and that made it feel real long before it was. But attention doesn't pour concrete. And when the money didn't arrive at the scale the dream needed, the land went back, piece by piece.

And it's worth saying what this story is not. It's not a story about a city that was built and then abandoned, like some of the others on this channel. Akon City never got far enough to be abandoned. It's a story about the gap between announcing a city and starting one, and how long a project can live in that gap.

The first stone was laid in front of cameras. The cancellation came in a short interview. In between, there was a field, a basketball court, and a village that spent years wondering what was coming.

Maybe one day, something will rise on those eight hectares. A resort, a hotel, a smaller version of the dream. If it does, it will be built the slow way: permits, investors, contractors and years of work that nobody films.

Out of eight hundred hectares of a future city, eight are left for something smaller. The rest is a lesson in how much a picture can promise, and how little it can build.
