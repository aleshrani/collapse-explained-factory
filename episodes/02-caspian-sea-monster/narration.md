## Hook

In nineteen sixty-seven, American spy photos caught something sitting on the Caspian Sea that shouldn't have existed. It had wings. It had ten jet engines. It was the heaviest aircraft on Earth. And it was built so it would never fly higher than the waves around it.

## Lock-in

The Americans gave it a nickname that stuck for sixty years: the Caspian Sea Monster. The Soviets spent fifteen years testing it. Then, in one bad year, they lost both the monster and the man who dreamed it up. Today, the monster is at the bottom of the sea, and its only completed little brother is sitting on a beach. So how does the heaviest aircraft in the world end up as a beach attraction?

## Point one · The man who hated water

[GAG: the architect rows a tiny boat as fast as he can; a hydrofoil zooms past and soaks him]
Alexeyev's view on boats was simple. If you can feel the water, you're doing it wrong.

The story starts with a shipbuilder named Rostislav Alexeyev. You see, Alexeyev's whole career was about one enemy: water. Water is heavy, it's thick, and it grabs onto anything that moves through it. The faster a boat goes, the harder the sea pulls back.

His first answer was hydrofoils. Little underwater wings that lift a boat's hull up out of the sea, so only the wings touch the water. His design bureau built fast passenger boats like that, and they worked. One of them, the Raketa, carried passengers up and down Soviet rivers at speeds normal riverboats couldn't touch. If you'd stood on a riverbank back then, you'd have seen a boat that looked like it was standing on tiptoes, rushing past with its hull hanging in the air. But even a wing in the water is still in the water. So Alexeyev asked the obvious next question. What if nothing touched the sea at all?

Here's the trick he wanted to use. When a wing flies very close to a flat surface, the air under it gets squeezed. It can't escape downward, so it piles up and pushes back. Pilots feel it every time they land, that soft floaty moment right before the wheels touch. It's called ground effect.

Alexeyev's idea was to live in that moment forever. A machine that never climbs, never lands, just rides the cushion of air between its wings and the water. Faster than any ship. Cheaper to run than a plane, at least on paper. And, as a bonus for a military that loved surprises, flying low enough to slip under the radar of anyone watching the horizon.

## Point two · Building the monster

[GAG: the architect reads the spec sheet; the line 'maximum height' says: a few metres]
Most aircraft brochures brag about how high they fly. This one bragged about how low.

So he built the biggest test he possibly could. They called it the KM, short for a Russian phrase that means model ship. It was about ninety-two meters long, almost the length of a football pitch. Fully loaded, it weighed around five hundred and forty-four tonnes.

To get something that heavy off the water, it needed absurd power. Ten turbojet engines. Eight of them sat up front, on little stubby wings near the nose, and their job was to blow air straight under the main wings at takeoff, to build the cushion. Once the monster was up and skimming, two engines on the tail kept it going. And when it was going, it could reach around five hundred kilometers an hour, just above the surface of the sea.

Alexeyev designed it, and he flew it himself. Picture that cockpit. You're in the heaviest aircraft ever built, the water is a few seconds below you, and the whole point is to stay there.

The takeoff alone would have been something to see. Eight engines at the nose howling at full power, blasting air down under the wings. A wall of spray rising on both sides. The hull dragging through the water, slower than you'd like, then faster, then suddenly lighter. And then the sea lets go. Not with a climb, like a plane. The monster just slides up onto its cushion of air and stays there, a few meters above the waves, heavier than anything that had ever flown, and refusing to go any higher.

## Rehook

And that's when the monster stopped being a secret. Because from space, a five-hundred-tonne aircraft parked on the water is very hard to hide.

## Point three · The monster on the photos

[GAG: two analysts squint at a blurry spy photo through magnifying glasses]
"Is that a plane?" "It's sitting in the water." "So, a boat?" "It has wings." "Fine. Write down: monster."

In nineteen sixty-seven, American spy imagery showed the machine near the Soviet town of Kaspiysk. It looked like a plane. It was far too big to be a normal plane. And it was in the sea, which planes generally try to avoid.

The letters on its side were K and M. So, the story goes, American analysts started calling it the Kaspian Monster, and the name slowly turned into the Caspian Sea Monster. The nickname did more for its reputation than any brochure could. For years, a machine most people had never seen was the scariest thing in the Caspian.

Now think about what a thing like that means to someone on the other side. A huge, fast craft that flies under radar, crosses water at five hundred kilometers an hour, and doesn't need a runway. On a map of the Cold War, that's a nightmare. Imagine you're the person who has to write the report about it. You've got a grainy photo, a size that makes no sense, and a vehicle that doesn't fit any category your office has a folder for. Is it a plane? A ship? A weapon? A trick? Whatever you write, somebody above you will want to know what it can do and when it will be ready. On the Caspian Sea, it was mostly a test bench. Declassified files mention it only in passing.

## Point four · The problem with flying low

[GAG: a form with two boxes, SHIP and AIRCRAFT; the architect ticks both and the form bursts into flames]
Ship or plane? The paperwork department never fully recovered.

Here's the catch Alexeyev could never fully escape. Ground effect only works close to the surface. And the surface is the sea.

On a calm day, the sea is a perfect runway. On a rough day, it's a field of moving hills. A machine that has to stay low has to stay low over waves, and every wave is a wall it can't climb over without leaving the cushion it depends on. Leave the cushion, and suddenly you're a very heavy, very thirsty aircraft with short wings.

Picture flying it on a grey afternoon. The forecast said calm. Now the swell is building, and every crest comes at you faster than you can think. Go lower, and a wave can clip you. Go higher, and the cushion fades and the engines have to work twice as hard. There's no shoulder to pull over onto. There's only the sea, and the sea gets a vote on every flight.

And those eight takeoff engines? They're only really needed for the first minutes, then they're dead weight, burning fuel and adding mass for the rest of the trip. A ship doesn't carry that. A plane doesn't either.

There's a paperwork problem too. Is this thing a ship or an aircraft? To this day, the international rules for craft like this are written by the maritime authority, the same people who regulate ships. So the monster was a plane in the sky, a ship on paper, and a puzzle for everyone who had to approve it.

## Rehook

For years, the monster kept flying anyway. Fifteen years of tests on the Caspian. And then came nineteen eighty, the year it all went wrong at once.

## Point five · The worst year

In January nineteen eighty, Alexeyev was testing a new, much smaller passenger ekranoplan. Something went wrong, and he was injured in the crash. He went through two operations. On the ninth of February, he died. The man who'd spent his life trying to lift ships off the water was gone, and the biggest thing he ever built was still out there on the Caspian, waiting for its next test.

A few months later, the KM went up for another flight. The pilot made an error at the controls, and the heaviest aircraft in the world hit the water hard. Nobody on board was killed. But the monster was wrecked.

And here's the strange part. It didn't sink right away. The KM floated on the Caspian for about a week. A five-hundred-tonne wreck, drifting, while nobody could figure out how to save it. It was simply too heavy to bring back. Then it went down. It has never been recovered. The monster is still out there, somewhere at the bottom of the sea it was built for.

## Point six · The little brother on the beach

[GAG: tourists take selfies in front of the beached warship; the architect photobombs with a peace sign]
Cold War superweapon, now a very popular selfie spot. That's quite a career change.

The idea didn't die with the KM. The Soviets built a successor called Lun. Smaller than the monster, but still enormous, and this one was openly a warship. Six large anti-ship missiles were mounted in pairs on its back. Only one was ever completed. It served with the navy into the late nineteen nineties, and then it sat at its base for years, slowly rusting.

On the thirty-first of July, twenty twenty, they finally moved it. Anyone standing on the shore that day saw something strange. A grey shape the size of a jet airliner, with missile tubes on its back, crawling down the coast behind tugboats. It took fourteen hours to tow it down the coast to the city of Derbent, where it was supposed to become the star of a new military park.

The park didn't happen, at least not then. Instead, the Lun ended up dragged onto a beach, damaged on the way, lying on its belly near the water. A Cold War warship turned into a place where tourists take selfies.

And now, the twist ending. In December twenty twenty-four, word came that the Lun would be restored. By October twenty twenty-five, most of the new paint was done, and the plan is to make it the centrepiece of the park in Derbent after all.

## Closing

In the end, the physics wasn't the problem. Ground effect is real, and the monster proved a giant could ride it. The problem was everything around it. The sea isn't always flat. Takeoff power you need for one minute, you carry for the whole trip. And a machine that's half ship and half plane has to answer to the rules of both.

Every few years, someone looks at those old spy photos and asks the same question Alexeyev asked. Why push through the water when you could ride the air just above it? It's a beautiful idea. It's just that the sea has never agreed to be flat for long enough.

Alexeyev built the heaviest aircraft on Earth so it would never have to climb. For a while, it was the most mysterious machine on spy photos. Now one of them is a wreck under the Caspian, and the other is getting a fresh coat of paint on a beach.
