## Hook

In twenty twenty-two, Saudi Arabia showed the world a city shaped like a single straight line. One hundred and seventy kilometres long. Two hundred metres wide. Five hundred metres tall. Built for nine million people, with no cars and no streets. Then a leaked internal audit put the cost of the whole NEOM project at eight point eight trillion dollars, finished around twenty eighty. By twenty twenty-six, work on the line had been halted until after twenty thirty.

## Lock-in

It's called The Line, part of a giant development in the northwest of Saudi Arabia called NEOM. Few city plans in history have been so bold, or so widely shared. Mirrored walls in the desert. Gardens hanging in the sky. A whole city where everything you need is five minutes' walk away. And within a few years, the plan was shrinking fast. So what happens when a city designed on a screen meets the desert, the budget and the laws of distance?

## Point one · A city as a ruler

The design was published in July twenty twenty-two by Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund. Here's what it described.

Two parallel walls of buildings, with mirrored outsides, running in a straight line for one hundred and seventy kilometres across the desert, from the mountains toward the Red Sea. The space between them, just two hundred metres wide. And the whole thing rising five hundred metres high. Taller than most skyscrapers in the world, stretched out longer than many countries are wide.

Inside, the city would be layered. Homes, schools, offices and parks stacked on top of each other, so that everything you need daily is close by. No cars, no roads. Instead, a high-speed railway running underneath the entire length. The plan said a trip from one end to the other would take about twenty minutes.

And the population target? Nine million people, eventually. That's more than the population of many entire countries, packed into one strip of buildings.

[GAG: the architect holds a pocket ruler up against the endless mirror wall, then a longer ruler, then gives up and sits down]
I brought a ruler. I'm going to need a bigger ruler.

Picture seeing that announcement for the first time. A video with dramatic music, drone shots over the desert, and then a glowing mirror wall slicing straight through the desert. It looked like a film set from the future.

And the promises went beyond the shape. The plan talked about a city powered by clean energy, with nature protected around it, and a way of living that would make the traffic jams and long commutes of normal cities a thing of the past. For anyone stuck in traffic that morning, it was a very attractive picture.

## Point two · Doing the maths on a line

Now let's do something the promotional videos didn't do. Let's do the maths.

One hundred and seventy kilometres long, two hundred metres wide. Multiply them, and you get about thirty-four square kilometres. That's the number in the plan, too. It's a calculation of the rectangle, not a measurement of anything built. And it's small. For nine million people, that's an extremely dense city.

But here's the bigger issue: shape. In a normal round city, the furthest you can be from the centre is the radius. Spread the same city along a line, and the furthest two points are one hundred and seventy kilometres apart. That's like the distance between two different cities. In Europe, it's roughly the distance from Amsterdam to Brussels. In the United States, it's a bit further than from New York to Philadelphia.

In a normal city, if one road is blocked, you take another. There are dozens of ways to cross town. In a city shaped like a line, there's only one direction to travel, and one main way to do it.

So what happens when you want to visit a friend at the other end? Or deliver bread from a bakery to a café a hundred kilometres up the line? Everything depends on that train working perfectly, all day, every day. If it stops, a part of the city is suddenly a very long walk away.

## Point three · A walk, a ride and a ruler

Let's follow two imaginary people. Sara lives near the middle of the line, on one of the higher floors, with a view across the desert through the mirrored wall. Her school, her office, her favourite café are all within a few minutes' walk, up or down a few floors. For her daily life, the plan works beautifully.

Now Sara's cousin lives at the far end, one hundred and forty kilometres away. Forgot your phone at your cousin's place? That's your Saturday gone. To visit, Sara has to get to a station, take the train, maybe change, and walk to the right building and the right floor. The plan says twenty minutes end to end. Even on a very fast train, it means a station, a platform, a ride, and the walk from the other station.

And for a delivery driver, a plumber, an ambulance, the city isn't a neighbourhood. It's a corridor. Everything has to travel along one spine. That spine has to be perfect.

And here's another problem with a straight line. Every service has to be repeated along the whole length. Hospitals, fire stations, supermarkets, schools. In a round city, one big hospital can serve everyone within a short drive. In a line, you need many smaller ones spaced along it, because the far ends are too far from the middle.

## Rehook

On paper, the line was a bold answer to urban sprawl. In reality, it was about to meet the things no rendering can show. Time. Money. And the desert itself.

## Point four · Shrinking the line

Then the numbers started shrinking. The goal for twenty thirty was one and a half million residents. Then it was fewer than three hundred thousand. In January twenty twenty-six, word got out that the whole line would be cut down and redesigned. And by May, work was paused until after twenty thirty, with a target of maybe a hundred thousand people.

Nine million. One and a half million. Three hundred thousand. A hundred thousand. That's not a city plan anymore. That's a countdown.

[GAG: the architect stands at a whiteboard, crossing out each number and writing a smaller one, until the last one barely fits in the corner]
At this rate, the next update will be one guy and a cat.

Picture that as a ruler. Nine million people needed one hundred and seventy kilometres of city. A hundred thousand people would fill only a small fraction of that. The rest of the ruler is still desert.

## Point five · Why it shrank

And then a leaked internal audit put a price on the whole NEOM project: eight point eight trillion dollars, finished somewhere around twenty eighty. NEOM says the number is being misread. Still, that's almost twice the size of the entire German economy, for one project in the desert.

[GAG: the architect tries to count on his fingers, runs out, takes off his shoes, runs out again]
I tried counting it. I ran out of fingers at about ten.

The reasons behind the shrinking are not mysterious. Costs that kept growing. Funding under pressure, as oil prices didn't stay high enough to pay for everything at once. And a long list of other giant projects across the country competing for the same money.

But there's a deeper issue that applies to every city ever planned from scratch. A city isn't one building. It's millions of decisions by millions of people about where to live, work and shop. A plan can draw the walls. It can't make nine million people choose to move into them.

[GAG: the architect fixes his hair in the giant mirror wall; behind him, the reflection shows only sand]
Five hundred metres of mirror in the middle of the desert. Perfect for checking your hair. Less perfect when the only thing in the reflection is sand.

There's also the cost of building tall in the desert. Five hundred metres is higher than most of the world's skyscrapers. Now build that height not once, but along one hundred and seventy kilometres. Every extra metre of height needs stronger foundations, more steel, more lifts, more pumps to push water upward. It's like building hundreds of supertall towers in a row and connecting them all.

Imagine you're one of the first families invited to live there. You'd be moving into the first section of a city designed for millions, with the rest still a desert. Where's your doctor? Your kids' friends? Your supermarket? The first residents of any new city pay the price of being early.

And a city also needs jobs, real ones, that pay well enough for people to want to move to the middle of a desert. People move to a place because there's work there. Work comes because there are people, customers and suppliers. Building that loop from nothing, in the middle of a desert, is the hardest part of any new city. The walls are the easy part.

## Point six · Proof versus promise

Here's something worth keeping in mind whenever you see a megaproject in the news. An announcement is not a city. A signed contract is not a city. Even foundations in the sand are not a city.

The only proof that a city works is people living in it. Children going to school. Shops making money. Trains running full. Until then, every number is a promise, and promises in the desert can shrink.

## Rehook

So is The Line dead? Not officially. It's paused, and it's being redesigned at a much smaller size. But the city that went viral in twenty twenty-two isn't the city anyone is building today.

## Point seven · What's left

The most likely future is something much smaller than the original vision. A short section, maybe. Fewer people. A different design. Other parts of NEOM, like resorts and industrial zones, may move ahead while the line itself waits.

And maybe that's the honest outcome. A city that starts small, proves it works, and grows if people want to live there. That's how most successful cities in history have grown. Slowly, unevenly, one street at a time.

The great cities of the world weren't drawn in one go. They started as a crossroads, a port, a market. They grew because people found a reason to stay. The Line tried to skip that step, and build the whole thing first.

## Closing

The Line was drawn from the top down. It was designed as one perfect shape. It promised a city of nine million in a strip of desert, all connected by a single spine. The shape made every distance longer, every dependency stronger and every delay more expensive.

The maths was always the problem. One hundred and seventy kilometres is a long way, no matter how fast the train. And nine million is a lot of people to convince.

Somewhere in the Saudi desert, there are foundations for a city that was supposed to reach from the mountains to the sea. And the next time you see a beautiful video of a city that doesn't exist yet, try this. Ask what it would be like to live at the far end. Ask how the bread gets there. Ask where the first ten thousand people will work. The answers tell you more than the drone shots.

For now, the line is a lot shorter than the ruler it was drawn with.
