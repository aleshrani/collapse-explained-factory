## Hook

The Soviet Union spent almost twenty years drilling a hole into the Earth, just to see what was down there. They got more than twelve kilometres down, deeper than anyone, before or since. And today, the deepest hole humans have ever made is hidden under a small rusty metal cap, about the size of a dinner plate.

## Lock-in

It's called the Kola Superdeep Borehole. It wasn't built for oil, or gas, or a secret weapon. It was built for science, and for pride, in the middle of a race between superpowers. It found things nobody expected. And then it hit a wall that wasn't made of rock at all. So why would anyone spend twenty years drilling a hole they could never climb into?

## Point one · The race to the bottom

[GAG: a Cold War scoreboard: the American arrow points up at the Moon, the Soviet arrow points straight down]
The Americans went up. The Soviets went down. Very literally.

In the nineteen fifties and sixties, the United States and the Soviet Union raced to the Moon, to space, to the bomb. But there was another race that most people have never heard of. A race to go down.

Nobody had ever seen deep inside the Earth's crust. Scientists had theories, drawn from earthquakes and from rocks that had been pushed up to the surface. But no one had ever drilled down far enough to check those theories directly. The Americans had tried a project to drill through the thin crust under the ocean, called Project Mohole. It was cancelled in the nineteen sixties.

That left the field open. If the Soviets could drill deeper than anyone, it would be a scientific triumph and a point scored in the Cold War at the same time.

The Soviets chose a different spot. The Kola Peninsula, far in the north, near the border with Norway and Finland. Old, hard rock, some of the oldest on the continent. And on the twenty-fourth of May, nineteen seventy, the drill started turning.

The goal was huge. Fifteen kilometres down. That's about one and a half times the height of Mount Everest, pointed the other way.

Picture the place. Flat, cold country near the Arctic Circle, with long dark winters. A cluster of buildings around a tall drilling tower. Inside, workers in heavy clothes, machines running day and night, and a hole in the floor that keeps getting deeper, centimetre by centimetre, for years.

## Point two · A very thin hole

[GAG: the architect kneels and tries to look down the hole; his head doesn't fit]
Twelve kilometres deep, and you can't even fit your head in.

Most people get one thing wrong about Kola. It's not a mine. It's not a shaft that people go down in a lift. Nobody has ever climbed into it.

It's a borehole. A very long, very narrow tube in the rock. The hole is only about twenty-three centimetres across. Roughly the width of a dinner plate. You couldn't fit your shoulders into it.

Think of it less as a tunnel and more as an instrument. A drill bit at the end of kilometres of pipe grinds through the rock. As it goes, the crew pulls up cylinders of rock, called cores, and sends sensors down to measure temperature, pressure and the rock around the hole. Every metre down is a new measurement of a place no human has ever seen.

And every metre is slow. When the drill bit wears out, which happens often in hard rock, the whole string of pipe has to be pulled all the way up, piece by piece, to change it. Then lowered all the way back down. At several kilometres deep, that alone can take many hours.

That was the job. You spend most of your shift not drilling, but pulling pipe out of the ground, unscrewing it, stacking it, changing a worn-out bit, and then feeding it all back in. Days of work for a few metres of progress. Over twenty years, that rhythm never really changed. It just got slower the deeper you went.

## Rehook

Year after year, the hole got deeper. And the deeper it went, the more the Earth refused to match the textbooks.

## Point three · What they found

The scientists expected the rock to change at a certain depth. Seismic studies had suggested a boundary several kilometres down, where granite would give way to a heavier rock called basalt. It's the kind of thing every geology textbook drew.

When the drill reached that depth, there was no basalt. The change the seismic data showed was real, but it was caused by something else: the same kind of rock, cracked and changed by heat and pressure. The textbook drawing had been an interpretation, and the drill showed it was wrong.

There was more. Reportedly, water was found deep down, far deeper than anyone thought free water could exist. It had been squeezed out of the minerals themselves and trapped by the rock above. The drilling mud came up bubbling with hydrogen. And kilometres down, the cores contained tiny fossils of microscopic sea creatures, preserved in the rock for an unimaginably long time.

For the scientist who opened that core tray, the moment must have been unforgettable. You're holding rock from a depth no person has ever reached, and inside it are the remains of tiny living things.

That's the real value of a project like this. A theory can be beautiful and still be wrong. Only a sample, pulled up from the real place, can tell you which. Kola was a machine for checking guesses about the planet under our feet.

## Point four · The record

In nineteen eighty-nine, the deepest branch of the hole, called SG-3, reached twelve thousand two hundred and sixty-two metres. More than twelve kilometres straight down. The deepest hole humans had ever made.

The plan was to keep going. Thirteen and a half kilometres by the end of nineteen ninety. Fifteen kilometres by nineteen ninety-three. They were more than eighty percent of the way there.

And then they ran into something they couldn't drill through.

Remember, every kilometre had taken years. The crews had lived with this hole for most of their working lives. The finish line was close enough to name.

## Point five · The heat

The wall was heat. The deeper you go into the Earth, the hotter it gets. The scientists had a prediction for the temperature at the bottom: around one hundred degrees Celsius. When they got there, it was about one hundred and eighty.

That doesn't sound like much compared to a kitchen oven. But think about what happens to rock at that temperature, under the weight of twelve kilometres of stone above it. It starts to behave less like a solid and more like thick plastic. It flows, very slowly, into the hole you just drilled. The hole tries to close itself. The drill bit wears out even faster. The equipment struggles.

Going further meant fighting rock that was harder to cut and also refused to stay put. Each extra metre cost more time, more money and more broken equipment than the one before. The crew could see where the numbers were heading. Every year, the hole moved forward a little, and every year, the heat pushed back a little harder.

[GAG: a thermometer at the bottom of the hole pops its top]
They expected a hundred degrees. They got one-eighty. Even the rock was sweating.

## Rehook

And then, at the worst possible moment, the other wall arrived. Not underground. Above it.

## Point six · When the money stopped

In nineteen ninety-one, the Soviet Union collapsed. The country that had started the project, funded it and taken pride in it, simply stopped existing.

The new Russia had much bigger problems than a scientific hole in the far north. Salaries went unpaid across the country. Factories closed. In that world, a research hole was an easy thing to stop paying for. Money for research dried up. Drilling stopped in the early nineteen nineties. For a while, the site stayed open for research. And eventually, the hole was sealed. A thick metal cap was welded over the top.

Think about how that must have felt for the people who worked there. Decades of careers, measurements, broken bits and small victories. The deepest hole in the world, still open, still with unanswered questions at the bottom. And the reason it stopped had nothing to do with science. The money simply ran out.

Picture the site today. Abandoned buildings, broken windows, rusted machinery, snow blowing across the ground. And in the middle of it all, on a concrete floor, a small metal lid with bolts around its edge. Under that lid is the deepest hole on Earth. Visitors who find the place sometimes stand on the cap for a photo, a few centimetres of steel between their boots and twelve kilometres of darkness.

## Point seven · The legend

[GAG: the architect puts his ear to the metal cap and listens; behind him a seagull screams]
No screams from hell. Just the wind, and one very loud seagull.

Of course, a hole that deep was always going to attract stories. The most famous is the legend of the well to hell. The story goes that drillers lowered a microphone and recorded screams from below.

It's a great campfire story. It's also a legend with no evidence behind it. The real borehole is too narrow for most of what the stories describe, and what the project actually recorded were measurements of heat and pressure, and samples of rock. What really happened is less spooky, and in a way, more impressive. Real people spent two decades sending instruments into a place hotter than boiling water, deeper than any mountain is tall.

## Point eight · Deeper or just longer?

You might read that Kola has been beaten. In two thousand and eight, an oil well in Qatar, called Al Shaheen, was drilled to a length of twelve thousand two hundred and eighty-nine metres. That's longer than Kola.

The difference is direction. Oil wells often curve sideways, following the oil. Al Shaheen went a long way horizontally. Kola went straight down. Longer isn't the same as deeper. Take two pieces of string of the same length. Drop one straight down a well, and lay the other across a field. Both are long. Only one goes deep.

And as far as we know, Kola still holds the record for the deepest point humans have ever reached inside the Earth.

## Closing

So did Kola fail? It never reached fifteen kilometres. The Earth's heat stopped it, and the collapse of a country finished it.

But it also showed something most megaprojects never do. That the maps we draw of the inside of our planet are guesses, and that sometimes, the only way to check a guess is to go and look. The textbooks were wrong about the basalt. The temperature was hotter than predicted. There was water where water shouldn't be.

And maybe that's the most honest result any megaproject can deliver. Not the record it promised, but a better map than the one it started with. Scientific papers still draw on what was measured there. The hole is closed, but what came out of it is not.

Twelve kilometres down, we found out how little we knew. And then we put a lid on it.
